\section{Scope and Reading Guide}
% =====================================================================

This document specifies, for every trainable model in the repository:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item the tensor that enters the model and its exact shape;
  \item every layer, with its weight shapes and its mathematical definition;
  \item the shape after each layer;
  \item what bypasses the encoder (the \emph{static skip}) and where it is
        concatenated;
  \item the output head and the inverse transform back to megawatts.
\end{enumerate}

Eighteen deterministic point models and four probabilistic baselines are
covered. The zero-shot foundation model (TimesFM 3.0) is deliberately
excluded: its weights are pre-trained elsewhere and its internals are not
part of this repository.

Two independent binary switches, \code{PJM\_CONGESTION} and \code{PJM\_LMP},
change the width of the feature set. All four combinations are trained, and
every shape below is therefore given as a function of the switch setting.
Numeric examples use the \code{\_cong} arm on zone \code{dom}, which is the
arm the best-scoring models were trained under.

\paragraph{Source map.} Section~\ref{sec:pipeline} corresponds to
\code{src/data\_crawler/} and \code{src/data\_processor.py};
Section~\ref{sec:features} to \code{src/feature\_engine.py} and the five
\code{src/*\_features.py} modules; Section~\ref{sec:point} to
\code{src/models/}; Section~\ref{sec:prob} to \code{src/quantile/},
\code{src/qrnn/} and \code{src/density/}.

\subsection{Cleaning and the training mask}

\code{clean\_and\_engineer} produces, per hourly row: cyclical calendar
encodings
\[
\big(\sin\tfrac{2\pi \cdot \text{hour}}{24},\ \cos\tfrac{2\pi \cdot \text{hour}}{24},\
     \sin\tfrac{2\pi \cdot \text{month}}{12},\ \cos\tfrac{2\pi \cdot \text{month}}{12}\big),
\]
a US federal-holiday flag, a weekend flag, and the training mask. The mask is
a per-\emph{month-of-year} $\times$ \emph{hour-of-day} three-sigma rule
combined with label availability:
\[
z_t = \frac{y_t - \bar{y}_{m(t),h(t)}}{s_{m(t),h(t)} + 10^{-6}},
\qquad
\texttt{is\_valid}_t = \mathbb{1}\!\left[\,|z_t| \le 3\,\right] \cdot \mathbb{1}\!\left[\,y_t \text{ observed}\,\right].
\]
On dom, $99.57\%$ of rows pass. A \emph{day} enters training only if all
24 of its hours pass; a failed day is still built into the matrix but is
dropped from the training set by the mask, so the test set keeps every day.

\subsection{The forecast timeline and the 24-hour blind spot}
\label{sec:timeline}

Every model in this repository answers the same question: \emph{given
everything known at midnight EPT on day $d$, forecast the 24 hourly loads of
day $d+1$}. That leaves day $d$ itself unobservable --- the lookback window
ends before it starts, and the calendar does not describe it.

\section{Feature Construction}\label{sec:features}
% =====================================================================

Features fall into two classes that are treated completely differently by
every neural model:

\begin{description}
\item[Sequence channels] change every hour and enter the encoder as a
      $(L, C_{\text{seq}})$ time series.
\item[Static features] take one value per sample. They are broadcast across
      the window in the stored tensor, but every model slices them back out at
      timestep $0$ and concatenates them \emph{after} the encoder. The encoder
      therefore never re-processes a constant 168 times.
\end{description}

\subsection{Sequence channels ($C_{\text{seq}} = 22$ or $25$)}

\paragraph{Thermal derivations.} With base temperature $T_b = 65^\circ$F,
\begin{align}
\mathrm{CDD}_t &= \max(0,\ T_t - T_b), &
\mathrm{HDD}_t &= \max(0,\ T_b - T_t), \\
\mathrm{CDDcum}^{(w)}_t &= \textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{w-1}\mathrm{CDD}_{t-j}, \quad w \in \{24,48,72\}, &
\overline{T}^{(w)}_t &= \tfrac1w \textstyle\sum_{j=0}^{w-1} T_{t-j}, \quad w \in \{24,72\}, \\
\Delta T_t &= T_t - T_{t-24}. & &
\end{align}
Heat index uses the NWS Rothfusz regression above $80^\circ$F and the simple
form below it; wet-bulb uses the Stull (2011) approximation
\[
T_w = T_c\arctan\!\big(0.151977\sqrt{\mathrm{RH}+8.313659}\big)
      + \arctan(T_c + \mathrm{RH}) - \arctan(\mathrm{RH}-1.676331)
      + 0.00391838\,\mathrm{RH}^{3/2}\arctan(0.023101\,\mathrm{RH}) - 4.686035,
\]
with $T_c$ in ${}^\circ$C and the result converted back to ${}^\circ$F.

\paragraph{Price transform.} Real-Time LMP has a $120\times$ right tail and is
negative in $\approx22\%$ of hours, so \code{log1p} is undefined and a raw
channel would be flattened by standardization. The channels use the signed log
\[
\mathrm{slog}(x) = \operatorname{sign}(x)\,\log(1+|x|),
\]
which compresses the tail, preserves the sign, and is defined on all of $\R$.

\subsection{Static features}

\subsubsection{Forecast-day calendar (6)}
Taken from the \emph{target} day $d{+}1$, not from the lookback:
\[
\Big(\sin\tfrac{2\pi m}{12},\ \cos\tfrac{2\pi m}{12},\
     \sin\tfrac{2\pi w}{7},\ \cos\tfrac{2\pi w}{7},\
     \mathbb{1}[\text{weekend}],\ \mathbb{1}[\text{holiday}]\Big),
\]
with $m$ the month and $w \in \{0,\ldots,6\}$ the day of week. Hour-of-day is
deliberately absent: it is implicit in the 24-dimensional output.

\subsubsection{Macro / multi-week context (4)}
Three seven-day windows ending at the cutoff position $c$:
$W_1 = [c-168, c)$, $W_2 = [c-336, c-168)$, $W_3 = [c-504, c-336)$.
Let $q_j$ be the $5$th percentile of \emph{preliminary} load over the
night hours $\{1,\ldots,6\}$ of $W_j$. Then
\begin{align}
\text{macro\_q05\_slope\_d1} &= q_1 - q_2, &
\text{macro\_q05\_slope\_d2} &= q_2 - q_3, \\
\text{macro\_energy\_ratio}  &= \frac{\sum_{t\in W_1}\tilde y_t}{\sum_{t\in W_2}\tilde y_t}, &
\text{macro\_lag168\_err}    &= \frac{1}{6}\sum_{k=1}^{6}\big|\tilde y_{c-k} - \tilde y_{c-168-k}\big|,
\end{align}
where $\tilde y$ is preliminary load. Metered load is never used here: it lags
by $\approx2$ weeks and would be leakage. This block is why the matrix builder
requires 504 hours of history before a sample can exist.

\subsubsection{Thermal state (5)}
With $d_1 = [c-24,c)$, $d_2 = [c-48,c-24)$, $d_3 = [c-72,c-48)$:
\[
\Big(\max_{t \in d_1} T_t,\quad
     \textstyle\sum_{t\in d_1}\mathrm{CDD}_t,\quad
     \max_{t \in d_1 \cup d_2 \cup d_3} T_t,\quad
     \frac{\sum_{d_1}\mathrm{CDD}}{\sum_{d_2}\mathrm{CDD} + 10^{-3}},\quad
     \overline{T}_{d_1} - \overline{T}_{d_3}\Big).
\]
These express what a causal rolling mean cannot: a daily \emph{extreme}, and a
\emph{direction} (the CDD ratio is $>1$ when heat is building).

\subsubsection{Forecast weather (31)}\label{sec:fcfeat}
Let $\hat T^{(0)} \in \R^{24}$ and $\hat T^{(1)} \in \R^{24}$ be the archived
forecast temperatures for the blind-spot day $d$ and the target day $d{+}1$,
both from the issue date $d{-}1$. Let $r_h$ be the mean observed temperature
over the lookback window restricted to true EPT clock hour $h$:
\[
r_h = \frac{1}{|\{t \in W : \mathrm{hour}(t)=h\}|}\sum_{t \in W,\ \mathrm{hour}(t)=h} T_t .
\]
The 31 features are
\begin{align}
\text{fc\_dev\_h}h &= \hat T^{(1)}_h - r_h, \qquad h = 0,\ldots,23 &&\text{(24 features)}\\
\text{fc\_d1\_cdd\_dev} &= \textstyle\sum_h \max(0,\hat T^{(1)}_h - T_b) - \overline{\mathrm{CDD}}^{\text{day}}_W \\
\text{fc\_d1\_hdd\_dev} &= \textstyle\sum_h \max(0,T_b - \hat T^{(1)}_h) - \overline{\mathrm{HDD}}^{\text{day}}_W \\
\text{fc\_d0\_max\_dev} &= \max_h \hat T^{(0)}_h - \max_{t \in d_1} T_t \\
\text{fc\_d1\_max\_dev} &= \max_h \hat T^{(1)}_h - \max_h \hat T^{(0)}_h \\
\text{fc\_2d\_cdd\_buildup} &= \textstyle\sum_h\max(0,\hat T^{(0)}_h - T_b) + \sum_h\max(0,\hat T^{(1)}_h - T_b) - 2\,\overline{\mathrm{CDD}}^{\text{day}}_W \\
\text{fc\_d1\_temp\_max} &= \max_h \hat T^{(1)}_h &&\text{(absolute anchor)}\\
\text{fc\_d1\_temp\_peak} &= \tfrac16\textstyle\sum_{h=14}^{19}\hat T^{(1)}_h &&\text{(absolute anchor)}
\end{align}
where $\overline{\mathrm{CDD}}^{\text{day}}_W$ is the window's CDD total divided
by $L/24$ days. Twenty-nine of the thirty-one are \emph{departures} rather than
levels, because 168 hours of observed temperature already state the level; three
absolute anchors survive because load responds non-linearly to temperature and
$+8^\circ$F on a $92^\circ$F base is worth far more MW than on a $62^\circ$F base.

\subsubsection{Congestion (8, when \code{PJM\_CONGESTION=1})}
Built from RTO-wide Day-Ahead transmission-constraint shadow prices on the
blind-spot day $d$. Let, for EPT day $\delta$,
\[
\ell(\delta) = \overline{\log(1+\text{sum\_sp})},\quad
\pi(\delta) = \overline{\log(1+\text{sum\_sp})}\big|_{h \in [14,20)},\quad
\chi(\delta) = \max_h \log(1+\text{max\_sp}),\quad
\nu(\delta) = \textstyle\sum_h n_h,
\]
and let $\mathcal{R}_w[f](\delta)$ denote the trailing $w$-day mean of $f$
\emph{including} $\delta$, with $w \in \{3,7,21\}$. The block is
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\ell - \mathcal{R}_7[\ell], \quad \ell - \mathcal{R}_3[\ell], \quad \ell - \mathcal{R}_{21}[\ell], \quad
 \pi - \mathcal{R}_7[\pi], \\
&\chi - \mathcal{R}_7[\chi], \quad \nu - \mathcal{R}_7[\nu], \quad
 \mathcal{R}_7[\ell] - \mathcal{R}_{21}[\ell], \quad \pi ,
\end{aligned}
\]
all evaluated at $\delta = d$. The last entry is the only level form retained.
The departure form is not cosmetic: a LightGBM gain breakdown put
$\ell - \mathcal{R}_7[\ell]$ at $3.31\%$ of total gain and the level $\ell$ at
$0.06\%$ --- a $50\times$ difference for the same underlying quantity.

\subsubsection{Day-Ahead price (10 when \code{PJM\_LMP=1}, 7 when $=2$)}
With $e$ the RTO-wide system energy price, $s = $ total $-$ energy the zonal
spread, and $\tau$ the total LMP (all under $\mathrm{slog}$, except the share,
which is formed on raw dollars):
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{PJM\_LMP=1:}\quad
&\bar e - \mathcal{R}_7[\bar e],\ \ \bar e - \mathcal{R}_3[\bar e],\ \ \bar e - \mathcal{R}_{21}[\bar e],\ \
 e^{\text{pk}} - \mathcal{R}_7[e^{\text{pk}}], \\
&\bar s - \mathcal{R}_7[\bar s],\ \ s^{\text{pk}} - \mathcal{R}_7[s^{\text{pk}}],\ \
 \tau^{\max} - \mathcal{R}_7[\tau^{\max}],\ \
 \mathcal{R}_7[\bar e] - \mathcal{R}_{21}[\bar e],\ \
 \tau^{\text{pk}},\ \ \tau^{\text{pk}} - \bar\tau .
\end{aligned}
\]
Setting \code{PJM\_LMP=2} swaps this for a drift-normalised block: four
$z$-scores $\big(x - \mathcal{R}_w[x]\big)/\mathrm{sd}_w[x]$ and three intraday
shape ratios, which are invariant to a uniform re-pricing of the day. That
variant exists because the price/load elasticity itself drifts --- mean RT
price per unit load moved $2.57 \to 5.76$ between 2024 and 2026 on dom, so a
model that learned the 2024 conversion under-reads load in 2026.

\subsubsection{The 2-D (tree) matrix}
The lookback is flattened column-wise, one column per (channel, lag):
\[
P = \underbrace{168\,C_{\text{seq}}}_{\text{flattened window}} + \underbrace{6}_{\text{calendar}} + \underbrace{S}_{\text{statics}}.
\]
Columns are named \code{\{channel\}\_h\{k\}} for $k = 0,\ldots,167$, where $k=0$
is the \emph{oldest} hour. Values are raw and unscaled --- trees are invariant

\subsection{DST normalisation}
Every target day is coerced to exactly 24 EPT slots
(\code{feature\_engine.\_normalize\_to\_24h}):
\[
y_h =
\begin{cases}
\text{the single observation at hour } h, & \text{25-hour day: } \tfrac12(y_h^{(1)} + y_h^{(2)}) \text{ at } h=1,\\[2pt]
\text{linear interpolation between } h{-}1,\,h{+}1, & \text{23-hour day, } h = 2,\\[2pt]
\text{as observed}, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
Without this the target would not be a fixed-length vector and $\code{out\_dim}=24$
would be wrong twice a year.

Three rules, all in \code{feature\_engine}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Chronological tail split.} $n_{\text{test}} = \lfloor 0.16 N \rfloor = 313$.
      The training pool is the preceding $1644$ samples; within it,
      $\lfloor 0.10 \times 1644 \rfloor = 164$ tail samples become validation.
\item \textbf{Embargo (14 days).} The last 14 samples of the train block and of
      the validation block are discarded. The macro features look back 504
      hours, so without the embargo a training sample's own feature window would
      overlap the validation period. Train $\to 1466$, validation $\to 150$.
      The test set is never trimmed.
\item \textbf{Validity mask.} Training samples whose target day contains any
      three-sigma outlier or unlabelled hour are dropped: $1466 \to 1461$.
      Validation and test keep every day, so the reported score is over the
      full period.
\end{enumerate}

Tree models take the same split but have no validation block, so their run tag
is \code{tail\_test0.16} while the sequence models' is
\code{tail\_test0.16\_tail\_val0.1}; both then carry the feature-set tag.

\section{Deterministic Point Models}\label{sec:point}
% =====================================================================

Eighteen models: nine architectures $\times$ \{direct, residual\}. All
eighteen share the input matrices of Section~\ref{sec:features}, the split of
Section~\ref{sec:split}, and the output convention below.

\subsection{Common output convention}

Every model emits $\hat{\mathbf{y}} \in \R^{B \times 24}$ in
\emph{standardized} target space. The evaluator converts once:
\[
\hat y^{\text{MW}}_{i,h} = \sigma_y\,\hat y_{i,h} + \mu_y ,
\]
using the persisted \code{y\_scaler}. The tree models are the exception ---
they are fit on raw MW directly and $\sigma_y, \mu_y$ never enter. The
residual models return a value already mapped \emph{back into} standardized
target space (Section~\ref{sec:resid}) so that the caller's inverse transform
is unchanged and the residual plumbing is invisible downstream.

\paragraph{Training objective (neural models).} Huber loss with
$\delta = 1$, on standardized targets, so the knee sits at exactly one target
standard deviation:
\[
\Loss(\hat y, y) = \frac{1}{24B}\sum_{i,h}
\begin{cases}
\tfrac12 (\hat y_{i,h}-y_{i,h})^2, & |\hat y_{i,h}-y_{i,h}| \le 1,\\[2pt]
|\hat y_{i,h}-y_{i,h}| - \tfrac12, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

\paragraph{Optimisation (neural models).} AdamW, $\eta_0 = 3\cdot10^{-4}$
(LSTM: $10^{-3}$), weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 32, gradient-norm
clipping at $1.0$, \code{ReduceLROnPlateau}$(\times 0.5$, patience $10)$ on the
validation loss, early stopping at patience 50, maximum 200 epochs. The
checkpoint written is the one with the lowest validation loss, not the last.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Tree models: XGBoost and LightGBM}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Structure.} There is no single model. Each of the 24 target hours
gets its own independent regressor, fit on the identical feature matrix:
\[
\hat y_{i,h} = F_h(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad h = 0,\ldots,23,
\qquad \mathbf{x}_i \in \R^{P},\quad P = 3750\ \text{(\code{\_cong} arm)} .
\]
So an XGBoost ``model'' on disk is a list of 24 boosters, $24\times1000 =
24{,}000$ trees in total. Hour $h$'s model never sees hour $h'$'s target, and
nothing ties the 24 outputs together --- which is exactly why the tree models
produce visibly rougher 24-hour profiles than the sequence models.

\paragraph{Boosting.} Each $F_h$ is an additive ensemble
\[
F_h(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \eta\, f_{h,m}(\mathbf{x}),
\qquad f_{h,m} \in \mathcal{F} = \{\text{regression trees of depth} \le 3\},
\]
built greedily by minimising the regularised objective
\[
\mathcal{J}^{(m)} = \sum_{i} \ell\big(y_{i,h},\ F^{(m-1)}_h(\mathbf{x}_i) + f_{h,m}(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)
                  + \Omega(f_{h,m}),
\qquad
\Omega(f) = \gamma T + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 + \alpha\|\mathbf{w}\|_1 ,
\]
with $T$ the leaf count and $\mathbf{w}$ the leaf weights. XGBoost solves the
second-order approximation, giving the closed-form optimal leaf weight and
split gain
\[
w_j^\star = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda},
\qquad
\mathrm{Gain} = \tfrac12\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda}
                              - \frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\right] - \gamma,
\]
where $G_j = \sum_{i \in j} \partial_{\hat y}\ell$ and
$H_j = \sum_{i \in j} \partial^2_{\hat y}\ell$.

\subsubsection{What a token is}\label{sec:token}

The word ``token'' is borrowed from language modelling and means something
different here, so it is worth stating outright before the layer walk.

\paragraph{One token $=$ one hour.} The sequence fed to the encoder is
$X^{\text{seq}} \in \R^{B\times168\times22}$. Position $t$ of that sequence is
one clock hour of the lookback window, and the token \emph{is} the 22 numbers
observed at that hour:
\[
x^{\text{seq}}_t = \big(\underbrace{\tilde y_t}_{\text{Load\_Estimated}},\
   \underbrace{T_t,\ \mathrm{AT}_t,\ \mathrm{DP}_t,\ \mathrm{RH}_t,\ \mathrm{SR}_t,\ \mathrm{WS}_t,\ \mathrm{WD}_t,\ \mathrm{WG}_t,\ \mathrm{PR}_t,\ \mathrm{CC}_t,\ \mathrm{ST}_t}_{\text{11 weather}},\
   \underbrace{\mathrm{CDD}_t,\ \ldots,\ T^{\text{wb}}_t}_{\text{10 thermal}}\big) \in \R^{22},
\]
already standardized by \code{scaler\_ts}. So a sample is a sequence of
\textbf{168 tokens}, each a 22-dimensional real vector --- $168 \times 22 =
3696$ scalars in all, the same numbers the tree models see as 3696 flat
columns. With the price channels on ($\code{PJM\_LMP}=1$) the token widens to
$\R^{25}$; nothing else about the tokenisation changes.

\paragraph{The embedding is a linear map, not a lookup.} There is no
vocabulary and no embedding table --- the input is continuous, so the
``token embedding'' is a single learned affine map applied identically at
every position:
\[
e_t = W_p\,x^{\text{seq}}_t + b_p \in \R^{64},
\qquad W_p \in \R^{64\times22},\ b_p \in \R^{64}
\qquad\text{(1472 parameters, shared across all 168 positions).}
\]
This is the only place the 22 physical channels are mixed before attention
sees them; attention itself mixes across \emph{time}, never across channels
directly.

\paragraph{Four things this tokenisation deliberately does not do.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No patching.} Each token is one hour, not a patch of several.
      PatchTST-style models group 8--32 steps into one token to cut the
      sequence length; at $L = 168$ the quadratic attention cost
      ($168^2 = 28{,}224$ scores per head) is negligible, so there is nothing
      to buy and a patch would blur the hourly resolution the 24-dim output
      needs.
\item \textbf{No \code{[CLS]} token.} Nothing is prepended, so there is no
      learned summary slot. The day representation is read off the
      \emph{last} position instead (step 4 below) --- the hour immediately
      before the cutoff, which after two bidirectional layers has attended
      to all 167 others.
\item \textbf{No padding and no attention mask.} Every sample has exactly 168
      real hours, guaranteed by the matrix builder (a day without a full
      window is dropped, not padded), so \code{src\_key\_padding\_mask} and
      \code{src\_mask} are both \code{None}.
\item \textbf{The static features are not tokens.} They are constant over
      $t$, so admitting them would mean 168 identical copies of the same 54
      numbers --- $9072$ redundant scalars for the encoder to re-read at
      every layer. They enter after pooling instead
      (Section~\ref{sec:transformer}, step 5).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Position is added, not tokenised.} A token carries no timestamp of
its own: \code{Datetime\_EPT}, hour-of-day and day-of-week are absent from the
22 channels. All the encoder knows about ordering is the fixed sinusoidal
vector added at step 2. That vector encodes \emph{absolute index}
$t \in \{0,\ldots,167\}$ on a geometric grid of wavelengths from $2\pi$ to
$2\pi\!\cdot\!10^4$; it does \textbf{not} encode ``this is 3\,pm'' or ``this is
a Tuesday''. The diurnal period of 24 and the weekly period of 168 have to be
recovered by the model from those sinusoids. That is a real design cost, and
it is paid back elsewhere: the window always ends at midnight, so index $t$
and clock hour are locked together by $t \bmod 24$, and the target day's own
calendar arrives through the six static features rather than through the
sequence.

\paragraph{Only the Transformers embed at all.} The three encoder families
consume the same 168 tokens but disagree about what to do with them first:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Encoder & Token unit & Embedding step & Position information \\
\midrule
Transformer / MoE-Tr. & one hour, $\R^{22}$
  & \code{Linear}$(22\!\to\!64)$
  & sinusoidal PE, added \\
LSTM / MoE-LSTM & one hour, $\R^{22}$
  & \emph{none} --- \code{input\_size}$=22$
  & implicit in the recurrence order \\
MSTNN / MoE-MSTNN & one (day, hour) cell, $\R^{22}$
  & \emph{none} --- 22 conv \emph{channels}
  & implicit in the grid axes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The LSTM feeds the raw 22-dim vector straight into its gates
(\code{nn.LSTM(input\_size=22, hidden\_size=128)}), so its first weight matrix
$W_{i\bullet}^{(1)} \in \R^{512\times22}$ absorbs the projection that the
Transformer does separately. MSTNN never forms a 1-D token sequence at all: it
folds the 168 steps into a $7 \times 24$ grid and treats the 22 channels as
\emph{convolution input channels}, so its unit of computation is a
(day, hour) cell and neighbourhood in \emph{both} axes is structural rather
than learned. That is the substantive architectural difference between the
three, and it is why MSTNN gets the weekly period for free while the
Transformer must infer it.

\subsubsection{Layer-by-layer}

\paragraph{(0) Input and the static split.}
The stored tensor is $X \in \R^{B \times 168 \times C}$, $C = 76$. The model
splits it on the channel axis at \code{n\_seq\_features} $= C_{\text{seq}} = 22$:
\[
X^{\text{seq}} = X_{:,:,0:22} \in \R^{B\times168\times22},
\qquad
s = X_{:,0,22:76} \in \R^{B\times54}.
\]
$s$ is read at timestep $0$ only, because those channels are constant along
$t$ by construction. $54 = 6$ calendar $+ 48$ statics.

\paragraph{(1) Input projection (the token embedding).}
\[
h^{(0)}_t = W_p\, x^{\text{seq}}_t + b_p,
\qquad W_p \in \R^{64\times22},\ b_p \in \R^{64}.
\]
Shape: $(B,168,22) \to (B,168,64)$. Parameters: $22\cdot64+64 = 1472$, shared
across all 168 positions --- see Section~\ref{sec:token} for what a token is
and why this is a linear map rather than a lookup table.

\paragraph{(2) Positional encoding.} Fixed sinusoidal, added not concatenated:
\[
\mathrm{PE}_{t,2i} = \sin\!\left(\frac{t}{10000^{2i/64}}\right),
\qquad
\mathrm{PE}_{t,2i+1} = \cos\!\left(\frac{t}{10000^{2i/64}}\right),
\qquad
h^{(1)}_t = h^{(0)}_t + \mathrm{PE}_t .
\]
Registered as a buffer, so it carries no gradient. Shape unchanged.

\paragraph{(3) Encoder, $\times 2$ layers.} PyTorch's post-norm
\code{TransformerEncoderLayer}, \code{batch\_first=True}, no attention mask ---
so attention is \emph{bidirectional over the 168 observed hours}, which is
legitimate because all 168 are in the past.

Multi-head self-attention with $n_{\text{head}} = 4$ and per-head width
$d_k = 64/4 = 16$:
\[
Q = HW^Q,\quad K = HW^K,\quad V = HW^V,\qquad W^{Q},W^{K},W^{V} \in \R^{64\times64},
\]
\[
\mathrm{head}_j = \softmax\!\left(\frac{Q_j K_j^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{16}}\right) V_j
\in \R^{168\times16},
\qquad
\MHA(H) = \big[\mathrm{head}_1 \Vert \cdots \Vert \mathrm{head}_4\big] W^O,
\ W^O \in \R^{64\times64}.
\]
Then the two residual sub-blocks:
\begin{align}
H' &= \LN\big(H + \Drop_{0.3}(\MHA(H))\big), \\
\FFN(H') &= W_2\,\Drop_{0.3}\big(\ReLU(W_1 H' + b_1)\big) + b_2,
   \qquad W_1 \in \R^{256\times64},\ W_2 \in \R^{64\times256}, \\
H'' &= \LN\big(H' + \Drop_{0.3}(\FFN(H'))\big).
\end{align}
Shape: $(B,168,64) \to (B,168,64)$. Parameters per layer:
$\underbrace{3\cdot64\cdot64 + 192}_{\text{in\_proj}} + \underbrace{64\cdot64+64}_{W^O}
+ \underbrace{64\cdot256+256}_{W_1} + \underbrace{256\cdot64+64}_{W_2}
+ \underbrace{2\cdot128}_{\text{2 LayerNorms}} = 49{,}984$; two layers $= 99{,}968$.

\paragraph{(4) Pooling.} Last-timestep, not mean-pooling:
\[
z_{\text{enc}} = H''_{:,167,:} \in \R^{B\times64}.
\]
The final position is the hour immediately before the cutoff, and it is the
one position whose attention output has seen every other position.

\paragraph{(5) Static skip.}
\[
z = \big[\,z_{\text{enc}} \;\Vert\; s\,\big] \in \R^{B \times 118},
\qquad 118 = 64 + 54 .
\]
This is the \code{feat\_dim} the head consumes. Note that it grows with the
feature arm: $110$ (base), $118$ (\code{\_cong}), $120$ (\code{\_lmp}),
$128$ (\code{\_cong\_lmp}).

\paragraph{(6) Output head.}
\[
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = W_{o2}\,\Drop_{0.3}\big(\ReLU(W_{o1} z + b_{o1})\big) + b_{o2},
\qquad W_{o1} \in \R^{128\times118},\ W_{o2} \in \R^{24\times128}.
\]
Shape: $(B,118) \to (B,128) \to (B,24)$. Parameters $118\cdot128+128 +
128\cdot24+24 = 18{,}328$.

\paragraph{(7) Inverse.} $\hat y^{\text{MW}} = \sigma_y \hat{\mathbf{y}} + \mu_y$.

\paragraph{Total.} $1472 + 99{,}968 + 18{,}328 = \mathbf{119{,}768}$ parameters
on the \code{\_cong} arm.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{LSTM}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

Identical plumbing to the Transformer --- same input split, same static skip,
same head shape --- with the attention encoder replaced by a 2-layer LSTM.

One difference in the tokenisation (Section~\ref{sec:token}): there is
\textbf{no input projection}. The 22-dim hourly vector goes straight into the
gates, \code{nn.LSTM(input\_size=22, hidden\_size=128)}, so the first gate
matrix $W_{i\bullet}^{(1)} \in \R^{512\times22}$ absorbs the $22 \to 64$ map
the Transformer performs as its own layer. Position is likewise never encoded:
it is implicit in the order the recurrence consumes the 168 steps.

\paragraph{Recurrence.} For layer $\ell \in \{1,2\}$, hidden width $128$, at
each of the 168 timesteps:
\begin{align}
i_t &= \sigma\big(W_{ii}x_t + b_{ii} + W_{hi}h_{t-1} + b_{hi}\big) &&\text{input gate}\\
f_t &= \sigma\big(W_{if}x_t + b_{if} + W_{hf}h_{t-1} + b_{hf}\big) &&\text{forget gate}\\
g_t &= \tanh\big(W_{ig}x_t + b_{ig} + W_{hg}h_{t-1} + b_{hg}\big) &&\text{candidate}\\
o_t &= \sigma\big(W_{io}x_t + b_{io} + W_{ho}h_{t-1} + b_{ho}\big) &&\text{output gate}\\
c_t &= f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot g_t, \qquad h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t).
\end{align}
Layer 1 takes $x_t \in \R^{22}$; layer 2 takes layer 1's $h_t \in \R^{128}$,
with $\Drop_{0.3}$ applied between the layers. Weight shapes:
$W_{i\bullet}^{(1)} \in \R^{512\times22}$ (four gates stacked),
$W_{h\bullet}^{(\ell)} \in \R^{512\times128}$,
$W_{i\bullet}^{(2)} \in \R^{512\times128}$.

\paragraph{Shape trace.}
\[
(B,168,22)
\ \xrightarrow{\text{LSTM}_1}\ (B,168,128)
\ \xrightarrow{\text{LSTM}_2}\ (B,168,128)
\ \xrightarrow{\ t=167\ }\ (B,128)
\ \xrightarrow{\Vert\, s}\ (B,182)
\ \to (B,128) \to (B,24).
\]

\paragraph{Parameters.} Encoder
$4\cdot128\cdot(22+128) + 8\cdot128 = 77{,}824$ (layer 1) and
$4\cdot128\cdot(128+128) + 8\cdot128 = 132{,}096$ (layer 2); head
$182\cdot128+128 + 128\cdot24+24 = 26{,}520$. Total
$\mathbf{236{,}440}$ --- roughly twice the Transformer, for a model that
scores slightly worse on both zones.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{MSTNN --- multi-scale temporal convolution}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

A simplified reproduction of MSTNN (Song et al., IJCAI 2025). The paper
reshapes a series into a year/month/day 3-D grid and applies parallel
multi-size 3-D convolutions; here the lookback is exactly $7\times24$, so the
grid is 2-D and the convolutions are 2-D. The paper's cross-entity
hypergraph attention is dropped --- there is one zone, so an inter-entity
graph is meaningless.

\subsubsection{The periodic reshape}

This is the whole idea. The 168-step sequence is folded so that
\emph{one axis is the day and the other is the hour}:
\[
X^{\text{seq}} \in \R^{B\times168\times22}
\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{reshape}\ }\ \R^{B\times7\times24\times22}
\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{permute}\ }\ G \in \R^{B\times\underbrace{22}_{\text{channels}}\times\underbrace{7}_{\text{days}}\times\underbrace{24}_{\text{hours}}}.
\]
A convolution kernel of size $(k_d, k_h)$ then covers $k_d$ consecutive days at
the \emph{same clock hours} simultaneously --- a receptive field that a
1-D convolution over the flat 168-step series would need a dilation of 24 to
reach.

The unit of computation is therefore a \emph{(day, hour) cell}, not a token in
a sequence (Section~\ref{sec:token}). The 22 hourly channels become
convolution \emph{input channels}, so there is no embedding layer and no
positional encoding: adjacency in both the day axis and the hour axis is
structural. The diurnal and weekly periods that the Transformer must recover
from sinusoids are here built into the tensor layout.

\subsubsection{Layer-by-layer}

\paragraph{(1) Three parallel convolution branches.} Kernels
$\mathcal{K} = \{(3,3), (5,5), (7,3)\}$, each with $40$ output channels and
``same'' padding $p = (\lfloor k_d/2\rfloor, \lfloor k_h/2\rfloor)$:
\[
g^{(b)}_{c,i,j} = \ReLU\!\left(
   \sum_{c'=1}^{22} \sum_{u=0}^{k_d-1}\sum_{v=0}^{k_h-1}
   K^{(b)}_{c,c',u,v}\; G_{c',\,i+u-p_1,\,j+v-p_2} \;+\; \beta^{(b)}_c
\right),
\qquad g^{(b)} \in \R^{40\times7\times24}.
\]
The three scales are local ($3\times3$: three days $\times$ three hours),
medium ($5\times5$), and full-week ($7\times3$: all seven days at once, three
adjacent hours).

\paragraph{(2) Global average pooling.} Each branch collapses to one vector:
\[
\bar g^{(b)}_c = \frac{1}{7\cdot24}\sum_{i=1}^{7}\sum_{j=1}^{24} g^{(b)}_{c,i,j},
\qquad \bar g^{(b)} \in \R^{40}.
\]
This replaces the original architecture's flatten-then-FC, which at
$\sim$1.5k training samples memorised immediately: a flatten would produce
$3\cdot40\cdot3\cdot12 = 4320$ features into the head instead of $120$.

\paragraph{(3) Concatenate.}
\[
z_{\text{conv}} = \big[\bar g^{(1)} \Vert \bar g^{(2)} \Vert \bar g^{(3)}\big] \in \R^{120},
\qquad
z = \big[z_{\text{conv}} \Vert s\big] \in \R^{174}.
\]

\paragraph{(4) Head.} $174 \to 128 \to 24$, same form as the Transformer's.

\paragraph{Parameters.} Convolutions
$40\cdot22\cdot(9 + 25 + 21) + 3\cdot40 = 48{,}520$;
head $174\cdot128+128+128\cdot24+24 = 25{,}496$. Total $\mathbf{74{,}016}$ ---
the smallest of the seven neural architectures, and on dom the second-best
(\code{mstnn\_residual}, MAPE $3.042\%$).

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Mixture-of-Experts head}\label{sec:moe}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

Three of the nine architectures (\code{moe\_transformer}, \code{moe\_mstnn},
\code{moe\_lstm}) replace the two-layer MLP head with a \code{RegimeHead}. The
encoder is unchanged in each case --- these are the same three encoders above
with a different decoder.

\subsubsection{Two-axis hard routing}

There is \textbf{no gating network}. Both routing decisions are deterministic
lookups:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Season} (per \emph{sample}): the target day's month maps to one of
      four seasons. Summer $\{7,8\}$, upper shoulder $\{6,9\}$,
      shoulder $\{3,4,5,10,11\}$, winter $\{12,1,2\}$.
\item \textbf{Hour} (per \emph{output index}): within each season, the 24 output
      hours are partitioned among three experts named \code{low}, \code{peak},
      \code{high}. The partition is season-specific.
\end{itemize}

active for any one sample.

\subsubsection{Forward pass}

Let $z \in \R^{B \times F}$ be the encoder output plus static skip
($F = \code{feat\_dim}$), and let $\kappa_i \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ be sample $i$'s
season index. Each expert $(s,e)$ is a two-layer MLP whose output width is the
number of hours it owns:
\[
E_{s,e}(z) = V_{s,e}\,\Drop_{0.3}\big(\ReLU(U_{s,e} z + a_{s,e})\big) + c_{s,e},
\qquad
U_{s,e} \in \R^{64\times F},\quad V_{s,e} \in \R^{|\Hset_{s,e}|\times64}.
\]
For season $s$, the three expert outputs are concatenated and permuted back
into hour order by a precomputed index buffer $\pi_s$:
\[
\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(s)} = \Big[\,E_{s,\text{low}}(z) \Vert E_{s,\text{peak}}(z) \Vert E_{s,\text{high}}(z)\,\Big]_{\pi_s}
\in \R^{B\times24}.
\]
The implementation evaluates all four seasons and then hard-selects:
\[
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i = \hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(\kappa_i)}_i,
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathrm{stack}\big(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(0)},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(3)}\big)\big[\kappa,\ \mathrm{arange}(B)\big].
\]
The three unselected seasons are sliced away before the loss, so they receive
no gradient. Gradient flows only to the selected season's three experts and to
the shared encoder --- which is what makes one forward pass train all twelve
heads over an epoch that spans the year.

\subsection{GARCHFormer (ARCHFormer3) --- variance-aware encoder--decoder}
\label{sec:garchformer}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

The ninth architecture, and the only one not written for this repository:
\code{src/models/\_garchformer/} is Moyi Guo's \code{archformer3}, vendored
from his \code{garchformer} repo. \code{layers.py} (709 lines) is byte-identical
to his; \code{pointmodels.py} differs in one line, the import path. The
architecture and every one of its hyperparameters are therefore his.

What was written here is the adapter,
\code{src/models/archformer.py} --- 179 lines that give the model this repo's
\code{train / predict / evaluate} signatures --- plus a nine-line hook in
\code{\_seq\_trainer.py} for its auxiliary loss. Everything else is shared with
the other eight architectures: the same 3-D tensor, the same tail-0.16 split,
the same embargo, the same scalers, the same evaluator, the same scoreboard row,
and the residual variant for free through \code{make\_residual\_model}. His own
data pipeline is deliberately \emph{not} used, because its
\code{forecast\_deadline\_hour: 23} predicts day $D$ from data through
$(D{-}1)$ 23:00 --- one day nearer than the task in
Section~\ref{sec:timeline}, and therefore not comparable.

\subsubsection{What a token is here}

Unlike the Transformer of Section~\ref{sec:transformer}, whose token is one
hour, \textbf{GARCHFormer's token is one day}. With
$\code{patch\_len} = 24$ the 168-hour window becomes
\[
N_{\text{patch}} = \frac{L}{\code{patch\_len}} = \frac{168}{24} = 7
\qquad\text{tokens.}
\]
So the whole encoder--decoder runs on a sequence of length \textbf{7}, and
each attention matrix is $7\times7$. That is the single most consequential
difference from the other five nets, and it is what makes the GARCH recursion
below affordable: it is a Python loop, and it runs seven times.

All $C$ channels go in --- there is no static skip. The 54 broadcast-constant
features are therefore presented to the tokenizer 24 times inside each patch;
that is upstream's design and is kept unchanged.

\subsubsection{Layer-by-layer}

\paragraph{(1) Patch tokenisation.} Non-overlapping day patches, flattened
across both the hour and the channel axis, then projected:
\[
X \in \R^{B\times168\times76}
\ \to\ \R^{B\times7\times24\times76}
\ \to\ \R^{B\times7\times1824}
\ \xrightarrow{\ W_{\text{tok}} \in \R^{64\times1824}\ }\ \R^{B\times7\times64}.
\]
$168 \bmod 24 = 0$ is asserted by the tokenizer. Parameters
$1824\cdot64 + 64 = 116{,}800$ --- 23\,\% of the whole model, and the price of
feeding all 76 channels rather than only the 22 sequence ones.

\paragraph{(2) Positional encoding --- learnable, not sinusoidal.}
\[
x \leftarrow x + P, \qquad P \in \R^{1\times7\times64}\ \text{a free parameter (448 values).}
\]
With only 7 positions there is nothing to extrapolate to, so a lookup table is
strictly more expressive than the fixed sinusoids of
Section~\ref{sec:transformer}, step 2.

\paragraph{(3) Encoder layer, $\times 2$.} Each layer produces a mean path
$\mu$, a variance path $\sigma^2$, and a fused representation for the next
layer.

\emph{(3a) Mean path --- causal multi-head attention.} With $h=4$ heads and
$d_h = 16$, over the 7 day-tokens:
\[
\tilde x = \LN(x), \qquad
Q,K,V = \tilde x W^Q,\ \tilde x W^K,\ \tilde x W^V,
\]
\[
A = \softmax\!\left(\mathrm{mask}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{16}}\right)\right),
\qquad
\mathrm{mask}(S)_{ij} = \begin{cases} S_{ij}, & j \le i\\ -\infty, & j > i\end{cases}
\]
\[
\mu = \LN_2\Big(\FFN\big(\tilde x + AV\big)\Big),
\qquad \FFN(u) = u + W_2\,\Drop\big(\mathrm{GELU}(W_1 u)\big),\ W_1\in\R^{256\times64}.
\]
The mask is \textbf{causal} --- day $t$ may attend only to days $\le t$. This is
the opposite choice from Section~\ref{sec:transformer}, step 3, where attention
is bidirectional over the window. Both are legitimate (all 168 hours are past);
the causal form is what makes the residual below a genuine one-step-ahead
innovation rather than a smoothed fit.

\emph{(3b) Synchronous residual.} The layer's own input is treated as the
observation and $\mu$ as its conditional mean, so the innovation is available
without a second pass:
\[
\varepsilon = x - \mu \in \R^{B\times7\times64},
\qquad
\varepsilon^2 = \varepsilon \odot \varepsilon .
\]

\emph{(3c) Variance path --- the GARCH block.} This is the paper's contribution
and the reason for the name. An attention layer first forms a causal,
data-dependent weighting of the past squared innovations:
\[
q = W_q^{\sigma} x,\quad k = W_k^{\sigma} x,
\qquad
\tilde A = \softmax\!\left(\mathrm{mask}\!\left(\frac{qk^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{64}}\right)\right),
\qquad
a_t = \sum_{j \le t} \tilde A_{tj}\,\varepsilon^2_j \in \R^{64}.
\]
$a_t$ is the ARCH innovation term, except that the fixed lag weights of a
classical ARCH$(p)$ are replaced by learned attention. It then feeds a
per-channel GARCH$(1,1)$ recursion over the 7 tokens:
\[
\boxed{\ \sigma^2_t = \omega + \alpha \odot a_t + \beta \odot \sigma^2_{t-1}\ },
\qquad \sigma^2_0 = \mathbf{0},\qquad t = 1,\ldots,7,
\]
with all three coefficients learned per channel and constrained at use time:
\[
\omega = \mathrm{softplus}(\omega_{\text{raw}}) > 0,
\qquad
\alpha = \sigma(\alpha_{\text{raw}}) \in (0,1),
\qquad
\beta = \frac{\sigma(\beta_{\text{raw}})}{\max\big(\alpha + \sigma(\beta_{\text{raw}}),\,1\big)} .
\]
The denominator is the interesting one: it is exactly the
\textbf{covariance-stationarity condition} $\alpha + \beta \le 1$ of
GARCH$(1,1)$, imposed by construction rather than by a penalty, so the variance
recursion cannot diverge. $\omega,\alpha,\beta \in \R^{1\times1\times64}$ ---
64 independent GARCH processes, one per embedding channel.

\emph{(3d) FiLM fusion.} The variance modulates the mean path by feature-wise
affine transformation (Perez et al.'s FiLM):
\[
s = \mathrm{softplus}(W_r \sigma^2),
\qquad
[\gamma \,\Vert\, \beta_f] = W_{f2}\,\mathrm{GELU}(W_{f1} s), \quad W_{f1}\in\R^{128\times64},\ W_{f2}\in\R^{128\times128},
\]
\[
h = \LN\big(\gamma \odot \mu + \beta_f\big),
\qquad
x_{\text{next}} = \LN\big(x + h\big).
\]
So a channel whose recent innovations were large gets its mean-path activation
rescaled, and the network can learn to trust $\mu$ less in volatile regimes.
That is the mechanism by which a variance model changes a \emph{point} forecast.

\paragraph{(4) Bridge.} A four-layer MLP that squeezes the embedding axis,
mixes along the \emph{temporal} axis, and restores the embedding:
\[
(B,7,64)
\xrightarrow{\ \text{fc}_1: 64\to1\ } (B,7,1)
\xrightarrow{\ \top\ } (B,1,7)
\xrightarrow{\ \text{fc}_2: 7\to32\ } \mathrm{GELU}
\xrightarrow{\ \text{fc}_3: 32\to7\ } (B,1,7)
\xrightarrow{\ \top\ } (B,7,1)
\xrightarrow{\ \text{fc}_4: 1\to64\ } (B,7,64).
\]
Only 680 parameters. It is the one place where information moves \emph{across}
tokens without attention.

\paragraph{(5) Decoder layer, $\times 2$.} Same three-part structure, with the
mean path replaced by cross-attention onto the encoder's last-layer $\mu$:
\[
\mu_{\text{dec}} = \mathrm{CrossAttn}\big(Q = \LN(x_{\text{dec}}),\ K = V = \mu_{\text{enc}}^{(2)}\big)
\quad\text{(no mask --- all 7 encoder tokens are visible)},
\]
then $\varepsilon = x_{\text{dec}} - \mu_{\text{dec}}$, the same GARCH recursion
on $\varepsilon^2$, the same FiLM fusion and residual. The encoder's
$\sigma^{2(2)}$ is passed in for interface compatibility but is \emph{not}
consumed by this decoder variant.

\paragraph{(6) Inverse tokenisation and the head.}
\[
(B,7,64)
\xrightarrow{\ W_{\text{inv}} \in \R^{1824\times64}\ } (B,7,1824)
\xrightarrow{\ \text{view}\ } (B,168,76)
\xrightarrow{\ \text{Linear}(76\to1)\ } (B,168,1)
\xrightarrow{\ [:,-24:]\ } (B,24).
\]
Worth stating plainly: the model \textbf{reconstructs the entire input grid} ---
168 hours $\times$ 76 channels --- and then reads the forecast off the
\emph{last 24 positions} of that reconstruction, through a single
$76 \to 1$ projection shared across all positions. The forecast head is
therefore tied to a reconstruction task, which is unusual and is one reason
the inverse tokenizer is the second-largest block in the model (118,560
parameters, 24\,\%).

\paragraph{(7) Variance output.} The last decoder layer's $\sigma^2$ is pushed
through the \emph{same} inverse tokenizer and head, then made positive:
\[
\sigma^2_{\text{out}} = \big|\,\mathrm{Head}(\mathrm{InvPatch}(\sigma^{2(2)}_{\text{dec}}))\,\big| + 10^{-6}
\ \in \R^{B\times24}.
\]

\subsubsection{Loss}

The point loss is plain MSE on standardized targets --- upstream's
\code{nn.MSELoss()}, not this repo's default Huber. To it,
\code{ArchFormer3Trainer} adds a variance-consistency term, reproduced here
through the \code{aux\_loss} hook:
\[
\Loss = \underbrace{\frac{1}{24B}\sum_{i,h}\big(\hat y_{i,h}-y_{i,h}\big)^2}_{\text{MSE}}
\;+\;
\lambda\underbrace{\frac{1}{24B}\sum_{i,h}\Big|\ \sigma^2_{\text{out},i,h} - \big(y_{i,h}-\hat y_{i,h}\big)^2\ \Big|}_{\text{variance consistency}},
\qquad \lambda = 0.1 .
\]
The second term is not detached, so it also pushes the mean path. Setting
$\lambda = 0$ recovers ARCHFormer2 behaviour on identical weights --- the two
share \code{layers.py} byte for byte, and v3 differs only in exposing and
supervising $\sigma^2$.

\paragraph{$\sigma^2$ is an auxiliary target, not an output.} It is computed on
every forward pass and used only by the loss; \code{predict()} returns $\hat y$
alone, exactly as upstream's \code{run\_point\_forecast.py} does. Its entire
effect on the delivered forecast is the shaping it applies to the shared
encoder and decoder weights during training.

\subsection{Residual models}\label{sec:resid}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

Nine of the eighteen models are residual variants. \textbf{Nothing about the
architecture, the features, the hyperparameters or the split changes} --- only
the target moves.

\subsubsection{The baseline}

For sample $i$, recover the raw-MW preliminary-load window and average it per
clock hour. In the 3-D path, channel $0$ is standardized \code{Load\_Estimated},
so it is un-standardized with the persisted sequence scaler:
\[
\tilde{x}_{i,k} = X_{i,k,0}\cdot \sigma^{\text{seq}}_0 + \mu^{\text{seq}}_0,
\qquad k = 0,\ldots,167 \quad\text{(raw MW)}.
\]
The window ends at a midnight cutoff, so window position $k$ sits at clock hour
$k \bmod 24$ and the seven values for hour $h$ are exactly $k \in \{h, h+24,
\ldots, h+144\}$:
\[
\boxed{\ b_{i,h} = \frac{1}{7}\sum_{j=0}^{6} \tilde{x}_{i,\,h+24j}\ }
\qquad h = 0,\ldots,23 \qquad \text{(\code{baseline='hourly'})}.
\]
The alternative \code{baseline='scalar'} broadcasts one number,
$b_{i,h} = \frac{1}{168}\sum_k \tilde{x}_{i,k}$; \code{'hourly'} is what is
configured and shipped. In the 2-D path the same numbers come straight out of
the \code{load\_estimated\_h*} columns, so tree and sequence residual models
lean on a bit-identical baseline.

Two properties make this legitimate: it is computed from the sample's
\emph{own} lookback (nothing is fit on the training set, so it cannot leak),
and it reads only preliminary load, which is published in real time (so the
residual models deploy on the same forecast path as everything else).

\[

\]

\code{residual\_scaler.pkl} \emph{beside the weights}, because predicting with
a different baseline mode than the one trained on would add back a baseline the
residuals were never measured against --- and would look like an accuracy
regression rather than a configuration bug.

\subsubsection{The design matrix}

Three specifications are implemented; \code{vanilla} is the one that ships (it
is the GEFCom2012 benchmark, the only one of the three with a frozen published
form). With $z_t = (T_t - \mu_T)/\sigma_T$ standardized on training rows and
$\mathrm{Tr}_t$ the trend in years since the record start:

\[
\underbrace{
\begin{array}{l}
\text{Vanilla:}\quad
y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_{\mathrm{Tr}}\mathrm{Tr}_t
    + \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{c=1}^{167}\gamma_c\, \mathrm{DH}_{t,c}}_{\text{Day}\times\text{Hour}}
    + \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{c=1}^{11}\delta_c\, \mathrm{M}_{t,c}}_{\text{Month}}
    + f(z_t) + \varepsilon_t
\end{array}}_{\text{180 calendar columns}}
\]
\[
f(z) = \sum_{p=1}^{3}\Big[
   \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{m=1}^{12}\theta_{m,p}\,\mathbb{1}[\mathrm{month}=m]}_{\text{12, full dummies}}
 + \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{23}\phi_{h,p}\,\mathbb{1}[\mathrm{hour}=h]}_{\text{23, one dropped}}
\Big] z^p
\qquad \text{(105 temperature columns)}.
\]

Total $180 + 105 = 285$ columns. Month gets full dummies and hour drops one:
if both blocks were full each would sum to $z^p$ across its columns, making
them exactly collinear. With month full, the bare $z^p$ main effect the paper
writes separately already lies inside the span.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrl@{}}
\toprule
Specification & calendar & temperature & total \\
\midrule
\code{tcube}     & 2   & 3   & 5 \\
\code{vanilla}   & 180 & 105 & \textbf{285} \\
\code{hong2014}  & 204 & 140 & 344 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
\code{hong2014} adds Holiday$\times$Hour (24 full dummies) to the calendar
block and, to the temperature block, $\mathrm{poly}_3(z_{t-j})$ for
$j\in\{1,2,3\}$, $\mathrm{poly}_3(\bar z_{t})$ with
$\bar z_t = \frac{1}{24}\sum_{j=1}^{24} z_{t-j}$, and Hour$\times \bar z_t$.

\paragraph{Two deliberate departures from the paper.} (i) The paper's Gross
State Product macroeconomic index has no PJM-zone equivalent, so a single
linear trend carries growth --- which matters on dom, whose load is rising on
data-centre demand. (ii) Polynomials are taken of standardized $z$, not raw
$T$: $\{1,z,z^2,z^3\}$ spans the same column space as $\{1,T,T^2,T^3\}$ so
the fitted function is identical, but $T^3 \approx 10^6$ in Fahrenheit is
badly conditioned.

\paragraph{Estimation.} $\hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \|X_{\text{tr}}\beta - y_{\text{tr}}\|_2^2$
by \code{numpy.linalg.lstsq} (SVD, minimum-norm). Minimum-norm rather than a
normal-equation solve because the 168-level Day$\times$Hour block loses rank
if a (weekday, hour) cell is empty in a short training window. The estimation
window is the 3 years ending at the split (\code{train\_years} $=3$): a longer
window averages away the recent growth and under-forecasts peaks (measured on
dom: perfect-weather MAPE $3.28\%$ at 3\,y vs $3.68\%$ on the full 4.66\,y).

\subsubsection{Scenario generation}

Three methods, all preserving the target index's own \emph{clock hour} and
remapping only the date:

\begin{description}
\item[Fixed-date] Take historical year $y$'s temperature and assign it
      date-by-date to the target calendar. $k$ years $\to$ $k$ scenarios.
\item[Shifted-date] The same, additionally slid by $\pm1,\ldots,\pm n$ days.
      $J = k(2n+1)$ scenarios. Because a shifted series is still a \emph{real}
      series, its autocorrelation survives --- the paper's stated reason to
      prefer this over resampling.
\item[Block bootstrap] Cut the target calendar into $m$-day blocks and draw
      each block, with replacement, from any of the $k$ years. $l$ scenarios.
\end{description}

The pool is stored densely as $\code{arr}[y] \in \R^{13\times32\times24}$
indexed $[\text{month},\text{day},\text{hour}]$, so a scenario is one numpy
fancy-index. The full cube is precomputed at
\[
\text{scenario\_preds} \in \R^{30 \times 81 \times 7800}
\quad (k_{\max}=30 \text{ years},\ \text{shifts } -40..{+}40,\ 7800 \text{ test hours}),
\]
and any $(k,n)$ configuration is the slice
$\text{years}[-k:] \times \{|\text{shift}| \le n\}$. Pool years must end
strictly before the test period --- using test-period weather to build
scenarios would be leakage.

\subsubsection{Percentiles and scoring}

The paper's empirical distribution function \emph{with averaging} is
transcribed rather than replaced by \code{np.percentile}, because at small $J$
the two differ and the published numbers would not be comparable. For $J$
sorted forecasts $x_1 \le \cdots \le x_J$, $p = q/100$, $i = \lfloor Jp\rfloor$,
$g = Jp - i$:
\[
\hat y_q =
\begin{cases}
\tfrac12\big(x_i + x_{i+1}\big), & g = 0,\\
x_{i+1}, & g > 0 .
\end{cases}
\tag{eq.\ 1}
\]
Pinball loss at level $p$:
\[
\mathrm{PB}_q(y, \hat y_q) =
\begin{cases}
(1-p)\,(\hat y_q - y), & y < \hat y_q,\\
p\,(y - \hat y_q),     & y \ge \hat y_q,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\mathrm{QS} = \frac{1}{99\,N}\sum_{q=1}^{99}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathrm{PB}_q .
\tag{eq.\ 2}
\]

\paragraph{Result (dom).} The $(k,n)$ sweep bottoms out at $(k{=}20, n{=}10)$,
i.e.\ $J = 420$ scenarios, with $\mathrm{QS} = 432.10$; fixed-date at
$k = 30$ gives $442.24$; the degenerate ``perfect weather'' floor (temperature
known exactly, $J=1$) is $269.10$. Ninety-five percent of the fixed-date
improvement is already reached well before $k = 30$, reproducing the paper's
flattening curve.

\subsubsection{Stage 1 --- sister forecasts}

The family is the Vanilla benchmark \emph{without} a trend (the paper's
eq.\ (1) has none --- it tracks growth by re-estimating daily on a rolling
window rather than by extrapolating), plus a recency block:
\[
y_t = \text{[Vanilla, no trend]} \;+\; \sum_{d=1}^{D} f\big(\tilde T_{t,d}\big) \;+\; \sum_{j=1}^{J} f\big(T_{t-j}\big),
\qquad
\tilde T_{t,d} = \frac{1}{24}\sum_{u=0}^{23} T_{t - 24(d-1) - 1 - u},
\]
with $f(\cdot)$ the \emph{same} Month/Hour-interacted cubic as the base
temperature term (105 columns each). Column count for one sister:
\[
\#\text{cols} = \underbrace{1 + 167 + 11}_{179\ \text{calendar}} + 105\,(1 + J + D).
\]

\paragraph{The eight sisters.} Two estimation-window lengths (2\,y, 3\,y)
$\times$ four selection schemes. Each scheme scores the same validation-year
forecasts differently and keeps its own $\arg\min$ over the
$(J,D) \in \{0..3\}^2$ grid:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
Scheme & Score & \multicolumn{2}{l}{dom selection} \\
\midrule
\code{pooled}      & MAE over all validation hours as one series & 2\,y: $(3,2)$ & 3\,y: $(3,1)$ \\
\code{per\_hour}   & mean over 24 separate per-hour MAEs         & 2\,y: $(3,1)$ & 3\,y: $(2,1)$ \\
\code{first\_half} & MAE on the first half of the validation year& 2\,y: $(3,3)$ & 3\,y: $(3,3)$ \\
\code{second\_half}& MAE on the second half                      & 2\,y: $(2,1)$ & 3\,y: $(1,1)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Selecting by \emph{partition} rather than by ``keep the four most accurate'' is
load-bearing. Measured on dom, the four lowest-MAE recency orders have mean
pairwise error correlation $0.963$; four chosen by different partitions sit
near $0.81$. Sister forecasts exist in order to disagree, so ranking them by
accuracy selects against the property the method needs.

\paragraph{Rolling estimation.} Everything is fit on $\log$ load:
\[
\hat\beta^{(i)}_{d} = \arg\min_\beta \big\|X^{(i)}_{\mathcal{W}(d,ty_i)}\beta - \ln y_{\mathcal{W}(d,ty_i)}\big\|_2^2,
\qquad
f^{(i)}_t = \exp\!\big(x^{(i)\top}_t \hat\beta^{(i)}_{d(t)}\big),
\]
where $\mathcal{W}(d, ty)$ is the $ty$-year window ending the instant before
day $d$, filtered by the same three-sigma \code{is\_valid} mask the point
models use, and $\hat\beta$ is re-estimated \emph{every day}
(\code{sister\_refit\_every} $=1$). Output: $(8, 20951)$ over $873$ days.

\subsubsection{Stage 2 --- quantile regression averaging}

For target day $d$ and quantile level $p = q/100$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Calibration window $\mathcal{C}_d$ = the $L$ whole days ending on
      $d-1$ (never $d$ itself, which has no realised load yet).
\item Select $\mathcal{M}_d$ = the $m$ sisters with the lowest MAE over
      $\mathcal{C}_d$.
\item Fit, per quantile level, on the $24L$ calibration rows:
\[
\hat\beta_{q,d} = \arg\min_{\beta \in \R^{m+1}}
   \sum_{t \in \mathcal{C}_d} \rho_p\!\left(\ln y_t - \beta_0 - \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}_d}\beta_i \ln f^{(i)}_t\right),
\qquad
\rho_p(u) = u\big(p - \mathbb{1}[u<0]\big).
\tag{eq.\ 5--6}
\]
\item Predict the target day's 24 hours and exponentiate:
      $\hat y_{t,q} = \exp\!\big(\beta_{0} + \sum_i \beta_i \ln f^{(i)}_t\big)$.
\item \textbf{Rearrangement:} sort each hour's 99 values ascending. Independent
      quantile regressions can cross; sorting weakly improves the pinball loss
      and is what makes a ``90\,\% interval'' actually an interval.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The solver.} Koenker's IRLS, transcribed rather than imported
(statsmodels is not a dependency, and the daily serving workflow reinstalls
requirements on every run). Starting from $\beta^{(0)} = \mathbf{1}$:
\[
w_t^{(k)} = \Big|\, \rho\text{-weighted residual}\,\Big|
          = \big|\,p\,r_t\,\mathbb{1}[r_t \ge 0] + (1-p)\,r_t\,\mathbb{1}[r_t<0]\,\big|,
\qquad r = y - X\beta^{(k)},
\]
\[
X^\star = X \oslash w^{(k)}, \qquad
\beta^{(k+1)} = \big(X^{\star\top}X\big)^{+}\,X^{\star\top}y ,
\]
iterated to $\|\beta^{(k+1)}-\beta^{(k)}\|_\infty < 10^{-6}$. Residuals are
floored away from zero at $10^{-6}$. Verified against sklearn's linear-programming
quantile solver --- identical pinball loss to six decimals, in-sample coverage
exactly $q$ --- and about $100\times$ faster, because the LP carries $2\times24L$
slack variables while this is a handful of $9\times9$ weighted least-squares
solves. \textbf{The seed must not be the OLS fit}: iteration 1 reproduces OLS
exactly, so an OLS seed satisfies the tolerance immediately and returns the mean
regression for every $q$. Warm-starting from the \emph{previous day's} solution
for the same $q$ is safe and is what the implementation does.

\paragraph{Selected configuration.} A validation sweep over
$m \in \{2,\ldots,8\}$ and $L \in \{91,122,183\}$ (21 combinations) picks
$m = 8$, $L = 183$ on dom (validation pinball $167.11$).
$L = 365$, which the paper also sweeps, cannot fit this record: a 365-day
window in front of a 365-day validation year pushes the earliest sister
forecast to 2023-08, whose own 3-year estimation window would begin four
months before the data does.

\paragraph{Design shapes.}
\[
X_{\mathcal{C}} \in \R^{24L \times (m+1)} = \R^{4392 \times 9},
\qquad
X_{\text{target}} \in \R^{24\times9},
\qquad
\hat Q \in \R^{99\times7800}.
\]

\subsection{Baseline 3 --- Quantile Regression Neural Network (Zhang et al.\ 2019)}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------

One network emits all 99 percentiles at once, trained on the summed pinball
loss. That is the point of the family: QRA needs 99 separate regressions where
this needs one fit.

\subsubsection{Feature pool and RFE}

The paper's pool is transcribed: date/time, holiday, lagged load, lagged
temperature.
\[
\underbrace{\{\text{load}_{t-h}\}_{h=48}^{840}}_{793}
\;\cup\;
\underbrace{\{\text{temp}_{t-h}\}_{h=0}^{840}}_{841}
\;\cup\;
\underbrace{\text{11 calendar columns}}_{11}
\;=\; 1645 \text{ candidates}.
\]
Load lags start at $48$, not the paper's $24$: this repo's cutoff is the
target day's previous midnight, so target hour $k$ sits $25+k$ hours after the
last observation and a lag valid for all 24 hours must be $\ge48$. A linear
trend is deliberately \emph{absent} from the pool --- the lag block already
states the current level, and a monotone trend column takes test-period values
that never occurred in training, which is the one input shape a network cannot
extrapolate.

Recursive feature elimination with an MAE-criterion random forest (40 trees,
\code{max\_depth} 12, 12\,000 subsampled rows, dropping $35\%$ per round) cuts
$1645 \to 100$, then the paper's manual add-backs
(\code{dayofweek}, \code{is\_weekend}, \code{is\_holiday}) are appended.
On dom that yields $\mathbf{101}$ features --- two of the three were already
selected --- split as $66$ load lags, $29$ temperature lags, $6$ calendar.
Selection sees \emph{training rows only}; the result is cached under a SHA-1
signature of the full specification so a changed setting cannot silently reuse
it.

\[

\code{bQRNN-400} is the paper's own \emph{control}: the same PReLU MLP with

$4587$ --- reproducing the paper's central claim.

\subsubsection{Standardisation, and a departure}
The paper normalises each sample to unit $L_2$ norm and \emph{then}
standardises by column. The first step is not reproduced: the feature vector
mixes MW with ${}^\circ$F, and dividing a row by its own norm makes the target's
scale unrecoverable from the input --- two days differing only in level become
the same row. Column standardisation fitted on training rows and frozen is used
instead. The target is standardised too; the pinball loss is positively
homogeneous, so scaling $y$ scales the loss without moving its minimiser, and
quantiles are equivariant under the affine inverse. The returned percentiles
are therefore exact, not approximate.

\subsubsection{Quantile crossing}
Crossing is measured on the \emph{raw, unsorted} output, since the paper claims
the joint output layer suppresses it on its own. The constraint score is
\[
\mathrm{CS} = \sqrt{\frac{2\theta}{N}\sum_{t}\sum_{q=1}^{98}\big[\max(0,\ \hat y_{t,q} - \hat y_{t,q+1})\big]^2},
\qquad \theta = \tfrac{1}{100},
\]
the spacing being $1/100$ rather than $1/99$ because the 1st..99th percentiles
are evenly spaced on $(0,1)$ with both endpoints excluded. Everything scored
downstream uses the sorted array.

\subsubsection{Deciles to a Gaussian (probit regression)}

If the nine deciles really came from $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ then
$y_q = \mu + \Phi^{-1}(q)\,\sigma$, so the points $(p_q, y_q)$ with
$p_q = \Phi^{-1}(q)$ lie on a line. Fit it by OLS over the quantile axis:
\[
\hat\sigma = \frac{\sum_{q}(p_q - \bar p)(y_q - \bar y)}{\sum_q (p_q - \bar p)^2},
\qquad
\hat\mu = \bar y - \hat\sigma\,\bar p ,
\qquad \hat\sigma \leftarrow \max(\hat\sigma, 10^{-6}).
\]
The probit grid is identical for every hour, so this is one vectorised
least-squares rather than $T$ separate fits. It \emph{is} an approximation, and
it discards any skew a base model captured --- the paper's defence is that
regional loads are close to Gaussian and that the mixture absorbs the residual
non-normality.

\subsubsection{Closed-form CRPS of the mixture}

This is what turns the combination into a quadratic program instead of a
simulation. Baringhaus \& Franz's identity gives
\[
\mathrm{CRPS}(F,y) = \E\big|Y - y\big| - \tfrac12\,\E\big|Y - Y'\big|,
\qquad Y, Y' \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F .
\tag{eq.\ 3}
\]
A difference of Gaussian mixtures is again one, and $\E|X|$ is additive over
components, so with
\[
\E|X| = \sigma\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,e^{-m^2/(2\sigma^2)} + m\big(2\Phi(m/\sigma) - 1\big),
\qquad X \sim \mathcal{N}(m,\sigma^2),
\tag{eq.\ 6}
\]
one gets the exact quadratic form
\[
\boxed{\ \mathrm{CRPS}(F_w, y) = \sum_{i}\sum_{k} \alpha_{ik}\,w_i w_k + \sum_i \beta_i w_i\ }
\tag{eq.\ 7}
\]
\[
\alpha_{ik} = -\tfrac12\,\E\big|Y_i - Y'_k\big|,
\quad Y_i - Y'_k \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mu_i - \mu_k,\ \sigma_i^2 + \sigma_k^2\big),
\qquad
\beta_i = \E\big|Y_i - y\big| .
\]
Note $\alpha_{ii} = -\sigma_i/\sqrt{\pi} < 0$, so \emph{every} diagonal entry
of $Q$ is negative.

\paragraph{Verification.} The closed form is checked against two independent
computations of the same number --- Monte-Carlo sampling of eq.\ (3), and
numerical integration of $\mathrm{CRPS} = 2\int_0^1 \mathrm{PB}_\tau\,d\tau$
over a quantile grid. \code{crps\_gmm.verify()} runs all three.

\subsubsection{The weight program}

Summing eq.\ (7) over the $T_3$ hours gives $Q = \sum_t \alpha^{(t)}$,
$c = \sum_t \beta^{(t)}$, and
\[
\min_{w \in \R^{15}}\ w^{\!\top} Q w + c^{\!\top} w
\qquad \text{s.t.}\qquad \mathbf{1}^{\!\top}w = 1,\quad w \ge 0 .
\tag{eq.\ 12}
\]
A weight returning zero drops its model, so \emph{selection and weighting
happen in one solve} --- the paper's stated reason for posing it this way.

$Q$ is indefinite, so the program is not convex as stated. The paper eliminates
the equality constraint and reports that the reduced matrix came out positive
definite in all its cases, without proving it. That is carried over as a
\emph{check} rather than an assumption: with $Q' = Q_{1:n-1,1:n-1}$,
$t = Q_{1:n-1,n}$, $Q_{nn}$ the last diagonal entry,
\[
Q_{\text{new}} = Q' - \mathbf{1}t^{\!\top} - t\mathbf{1}^{\!\top} + Q_{nn}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\!\top},
\tag{eq.\ 24}
\]
and \code{solve\_crps} returns $\lambda_{\min}(Q_{\text{new}})$ alongside the
weights, flagging whether the solution is \emph{certified global} or merely a
local optimum. The solver is SLSQP with 8 multi-starts (uniform plus 7
Dirichlet draws); if the certificate is positive the extra starts are
redundant, and if it is not they are the only defence.

\subsubsection{Two comparison schemes}
\code{solve\_mape} minimises $\frac1T\sum_t|y_t - w^{\!\top}\mu_t|/y_t$ on the
same simplex, and \code{simple\_average} sets $w_i = 1/15$. The point of
carrying them is the paper's own observation: a MAPE-guided combination knows
nothing about the shape of the density, so it can win on MAPE while losing on
CRPS.

\paragraph{Result (dom).} CRPS-guided $958.5$, MAPE-guided $965.8$, simple
average $956.7$, best individual member (XGB4) $\mathbf{929.1}$. The
combination does \emph{not} beat its best member here --- a reportable negative
result, and consistent with $T_3$ being only 56 days against the paper's
longer weight-optimisation window.

\subsubsection{Scoring the mixture}
The mixture CDF has no closed-form inverse, so pinball, Winkler and coverage
are read off a grid of 4096 points spanning $\pm6$ standard deviations of the
widest component:
\[
F(z) = \sum_i w_i\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{z - \mu_i}{\sigma_i}\right),
\qquad
\hat y_q = \text{interp}\big(q;\ F(z),\ z\big).
\]
The point forecast is the mixture mean $w^{\!\top}\mu$.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}\label{sec:metrics}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Point metrics}

With $y$ the metered load and $\hat y$ the forecast, flattened over all
test days $\times$ 24 hours ($313\times24 = 7512$ values on dom):

\begin{align}
\mathrm{MAPE} &= \frac{100}{n}\sum_{i}\frac{|y_i - \hat y_i|}{|y_i|}\ \ [\%] &
\mathrm{MAE}  &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_i |y_i - \hat y_i|\ \ [\mathrm{MW}] \\
\mathrm{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\tfrac1n\sum_i (y_i-\hat y_i)^2}\ \ [\mathrm{MW}] &
\mathrm{ME}   &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat y_i - y_i)\ \ [\mathrm{MW}]
\end{align}

\paragraph{Peak MAPE.} MAPE restricted to the top decile of \emph{true} load:
$\mathrm{MAPE}$ over $\{i : y_i \ge \mathrm{Q}_{0.9}(y)\}$. This is the number
a system operator actually cares about, and it separates models that global
MAPE ranks as equivalent.

\paragraph{BRS --- Binned Residual Slope.} A single number for
\emph{load-dependent bias}, which ME cannot express because opposite biases at
low and high load cancel. Bin the flattened test set into ten equal-count bins
by true load; let $\bar y_j$ and $\bar e_j = \overline{(\hat y - y)}_j$ be the
per-bin means; then
\[
\mathrm{BRS} = \text{the OLS slope of } \bar e_j \text{ on } \bar y_j
\qquad [\mathrm{MW}/\mathrm{MW}].
\]
$\mathrm{BRS} > 0$ means the model over-predicts at peak; $<0$ under-predicts
at peak; $\approx 0$ is unbiased across the load range. Every model in this
repository has $\mathrm{BRS} < 0$ --- they all shave the peak --- and the
magnitude ranges from $-0.03$ (best) to $-0.20$ (plain XGBoost).

\subsection{Probabilistic metrics}

\paragraph{Pinball / quantile score.} Eq.\ (2) of Section~\ref{sec:prob},
averaged over the 99 levels and all hours. Lower is better.

\paragraph{Winkler (interval) score.} For a central $c\%$ interval
$[L,U]$ with $\alpha = 1 - c/100$ and width $\delta = U - L$:
\[
W_c(y) =
\begin{cases}
\delta, & L \le y \le U,\\
\delta + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}(L-y), & y < L,\\
\delta + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}(y-U), & y > U.
\end{cases}
\]
Pinball alone cannot distinguish ``covers everything because it is enormous''
from ``covers everything and is tight''. Winkler charges for width on every
hour and adds a $1/\alpha$-scaled penalty only on a miss, so both attributes
are scored together.

\paragraph{CRPS.} Computed from a quantile function by
$\mathrm{CRPS} = 2\int_0^1 \mathrm{PB}_\tau\,d\tau$ (trapezoid over the
supplied grid), so that all four baselines can be scored on it --- it is the
first metric all the papers share. For the Gaussian-mixture baseline the
closed form of Section~\ref{sec:prob} is available and agrees.

\paragraph{Coverage.} For each nominal level $c$, the empirical fraction of
hours inside $[L,U]$, together with the split between below and above and the
mean width. Neither source paper reports it; it is kept because it is the first
thing a reader asks, and it is where the scenario baseline visibly fails --- its
nominal $98\%$ interval covers $77\%$.

\subsection{What GARCHFormer bought}\label{sec:garchformer-result}

The variance-aware architecture does \emph{not} win on average error --- it is
23rd of the 66 point runs on dom. What it wins is the two metrics that describe
behaviour \emph{at the peak}, and it wins both outright:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrr@{}}
\toprule
Rank & Model / arm & MAPE\,\% & peak MAPE\,\% & BRS \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{5}{@{}l}{\emph{by peak MAPE --- the top of the board}} \\
1 & \code{archformer\_residual} \code{\_cong}      & 3.263 & \textbf{3.855} & $-0.0198$ \\
2 & \code{lstm\_residual} \code{''}                & 3.202 & 3.907 & $-0.0347$ \\
3 & \code{transformer\_residual} \code{\_cong}     & 3.018 & 3.937 & $-0.0553$ \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{5}{@{}l}{\emph{by $|\mathrm{BRS}|$ --- flattest bias across the load range}} \\
1 & \code{archformer\_residual} \code{\_cong}      & 3.263 & 3.855 & $\mathbf{-0.0198}$ \\
2 & \code{moe\_mstnn} \code{\_cong\_lmp}           & 4.889 & 5.359 & $-0.0305$ \\
3 & \code{lstm\_residual} \code{''}                & 3.202 & 3.907 & $-0.0347$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Section~\ref{sec:metrics} noted that every model in this repository has
$\mathrm{BRS} < 0$ --- they all shave the peak. GARCHFormer shaves it least, by
a clear margin: $-0.0198$ against a previous best of $-0.0305$, and against
$-0.0553$ for the model that beats it on overall MAPE.

That is the result the architecture predicts. Load volatility is highest at the
peak; the GARCH recursion of Section~\ref{sec:garchformer}, step 3c gives the
network an explicit, per-channel estimate of that volatility, and the FiLM
fusion of step 3d lets it rescale the mean path accordingly. A model that can
represent ``this hour is in a high-variance regime'' has a mechanism for not
regressing the peak toward the mean, which is exactly what the other eight
architectures lack.

\paragraph{Caveats.} One 50-epoch run per variant, on one zone
(\code{dom}) and one arm (\code{\_cong}), with no tuning of any kind --- the
architecture hyperparameters are the author's and $\lambda_{\text{cons}}$ was
never swept. bge and the remaining three arms have not been run. The claim
supported by the evidence is a single-arm, single-zone one; it is suggestive,
not established.
