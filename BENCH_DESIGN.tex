\noindent
Five modules from the first draft are gone. \texttt{qra.py} went with Model C

\medskip
\noindent

\subsection{The Dataset object}

The unit is a \textbf{day}, not a step. Everything downstream addresses days by the position
of their first step, and expands to steps only when a matrix is built --- which is what makes
the day-set rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:dayset} expressible at all.

\begin{code}
class Dataset:
    name            'spain' | 'ercot' | 'elia'
    index           pd.DatetimeIndex, UTC, verified strictly regular
    y               np.ndarray, the target column
    step_minutes    15 or 60          steps_per_hour   4 or 1
    H               steps in a target day        24 or 96
    L               steps in the lookback window  168 or 672
    G               steps from the deadline to the target day's first step
    lag_min         = G + H - 1       lag_max          = L - 1 + G

    day_starts()          positions of the first step of every usable day
    splits()              {'train','val','test'} -> day-start positions
    steps_of(days)        (n_days * H,) step positions, chronological
    standardiser(days)    (mean, sd) of the target over those days
\end{code}

\noindent
Construction rejects the record outright unless the timestamp grid is \emph{strictly} regular
--- a single value of \texttt{diff}, equal to the configured granularity, and no duplicates ---
because that is what allows every lag to be a positional shift rather than a timestamp join.
All three datasets pass: \texttt{spain} 35{,}039 hourly stamps, \texttt{ercot} 16{,}958,
\texttt{elia} 359{,}228 quarter-hourly, no gaps and no duplicates in any of them. Unlike PJM
there is no DST irregularity to normalise.

\medskip
\noindent
Column classes (\texttt{past\_cols} / \texttt{known\_cols} / \texttt{aux\_cols}) and the
alignment probe are not in \texttt{data.py}. Model A uses none of them
(Sec.~\ref{sec:densityfeat}), so they are deferred to whichever of Models B and C needs them
first rather than built speculatively.

\subsection{Execution flow}

\begin{code}
scripts/train_bench_density.py --dataset spain --seeds 0 1 2 [--workers N] [--models ...]
   |
   +-- data.load("spain")         JSON + CSV -> validated Dataset
   +-- ds.splits()                day sets for train / val / test
   |
   +-- for each seed:
   |      standardiser(train)     (mean, sd) -> z-score the whole target series
   |      density.build(...)      (X, y) for T1..T4, every step of every usable day
   |      fit 15 base models on T1, predict on T2+T3+T4   [process pool]

\noindent
Every model emits the same object --- a \texttt{(3, n\_test)} array of sorted quantiles, the

three.

\subsection{The day set}
\label{sec:dayset}

A target day is usable when three conditions hold together:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item all $H$ of its steps are present in the record;
  \item the whole lookback window ending at its deadline is present too;
  \item all $H$ of its steps lie inside the split's date range.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Condition 3 is the one that is easy to get wrong, and it is what makes \texttt{elia}'s test set
746 days rather than 747: \texttt{test\_end} is \texttt{23:00}, but a 15-minute day's last step
is \texttt{23:45}, so the final day is incomplete and is dropped rather than scored on
three-quarters of its steps.

\medskip
\noindent
A \emph{sample} is still one target step, as in all three papers --- days are the unit of
admission, steps the unit of fitting. A day-level layout would leave \texttt{ercot} with 416
training rows, which none of the three models can be fitted on.

\subsection{The lag window}
\label{sec:lagwindow}

The first draft had a one-sided \emph{floor}. The chapter's window is anchored at the deadline
and has a fixed length, so the bound is two-sided. Write $G$ for the number of steps from the
deadline step to the target day's first step. A lag $k$, counted back from a target step, is
observable for \emph{every} step of the day and lies inside the window exactly when
\begin{equation}
  G + H - 1 \;\leq\; k \;\leq\; L - 1 + G .
  \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}

\noindent
The lower bound is the old floor: the day's last step is $H-1$ after its first, which is itself
$G$ after the deadline. The upper bound is new and is the one the draft missed --- a lag deeper
than $L-1+G$ reaches back past the start of the lookback window, so using it would quietly give
the model a longer history than the protocol allows.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l l l@{}}
\toprule
 & deadline & $G$ & $H$ & $L$ & window \\
\midrule
\texttt{elia}  & $(D{-}1)$ 23:00 & 4  & 96 & 672 & $[99,\ 675]$ \\
\texttt{ercot} & $(D{-}1)$ 09:00 & 15 & 24 & 168 & $[38,\ 182]$ \\
\texttt{spain} & $(D{-}1)$ 23:00 & 1  & 24 & 168 & $[24,\ 168]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
\texttt{elia}'s lower bound is 99 rather than a round 96 because the final 15-minute step runs
45 minutes past the whole day. \texttt{ercot}'s deadline of 9 gives a blind spot of 15--38
hours, far wider than the other two; that is its market rule (the day-ahead market closes the
previous morning), not an error.

\medskip
\noindent
Eq.~\eqref{eq:window} is the single rule every model's feature set is filtered through. For
Model A it costs one lag on two of the three datasets (Sec.~\ref{sec:densityfeat}); for Model B
it is the change that makes the lazy matrix builder unnecessary.

\subsection{Feature availability}
\label{sec:avail}

One rule, shared by all three models:

\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{5.5cm} l L@{}}
\toprule
column class & usable lags & reason \\
\midrule
\texttt{target\_col} & inside Eq.~\eqref{eq:window} & realised value, known only after the cutoff, and only back to the start of the window \\
\texttt{past\_cols} (\texttt{actual\_cols} minus target) & inside Eq.~\eqref{eq:window} & same \\
\texttt{known\_cols} (\texttt{forecast\_cols}) & \textbf{lag 0} & day-ahead forecast, describes the target day, published before the cutoff \\

\medskip
\noindent
\texttt{target\_col} also appearing inside \texttt{actual\_cols} is harmless --- it is handled
as the target.

\section{Model A --- density combination (Li, Wang \& Zhang 2020)}

\noindent
\textbf{Built and unit-tested; not yet run end to end.}
\texttt{src/bench/density.py} $+$ \texttt{train\_bench\_density.py}. The numerical core is
imported unchanged from \texttt{src/density} --- the fifteen base specifications, the probit
fit, the closed-form mixture CRPS and the simplex QP. What is written new is only what the
protocol changes: the feature assembly, the T1--T4 mapping, the reporting space, and the
three-level output.

\subsection{Architecture}

\begin{codetiny}
15 base learners, three families of five

  GPR  x5   ARD Matern 3/2 + RBF + White kernel
            diversity: a different 2000-point random subsample of T1 (seeds 100-104)

  QRNN x5   diversity: architecture x feature subset x width
            NN1 bn/all/64    NN2 bn/lags/128   NN3 l2/all/64
            NN4 l2/all/128   NN5 l2/recent/96
            subsets: all, lags = the lag columns only, recent = short lags + calendar

  QRGB x5   diversity: max_depth / min_child_weight / subsample / colsample_bytree
            XGB1 4/1/0.9/0.9    XGB2 6/5/0.8/0.8    XGB3 8/10/0.7/0.7
            XGB4 3/20/0.9/0.6   XGB5 6/2/0.6/1.0
       |
       +--- GPR emits a Gaussian (mu, sigma) natively
       +--- QRNN / QRGB emit 9 deciles -> Gaussian by probit regression
       |
       v
  Gaussian mixture   F = sum_i w_i F_i
       |
       v
  weights minimise the CRPS; for a Gaussian mixture it is closed form, hence
  quadratic in w  ->  a small QP on the simplex, not a simulation.
  w_i = 0 drops model i, so selection and weighting happen in one solve.
       |
       v
  reported alongside: MAPE-guided weights, simple average, best single model on T3
\end{codetiny}

\noindent
The probit conversion: if $y_q = \mu + \Phi^{-1}(q)\,\sigma$, then an OLS fit of $y_q$ on
$\Phi^{-1}(q)$ returns the intercept as $\mu$ and the slope as $\sigma$.

\medskip
\noindent
The closed form is the Baringhaus--Franz identity,
\begin{equation*}
  \mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) \;=\; \mathbb{E}|Y - y| - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}|Y - Y'|
  \;=\; \sum_i \sum_k \alpha_{i,k}\, w_i w_k \;+\; \sum_i \beta_i w_i ,
\end{equation*}
so the weights solve
$\min_{w} w^{\top} Q w + c^{\top} w$ subject to $\mathbf{1}^{\top}w = 1,\ w \geq 0$.
$Q$ is indefinite ($\alpha_{i,i} = -\sigma_i/\sqrt{\pi}$); the paper eliminates the equality
constraint and reports the reduced matrix positive definite, which is computed here as a
certificate rather than assumed. Multi-start SLSQP either way.

\subsection{Features --- the paper's list, no exogenous variables}
\label{sec:densityfeat}

\texttt{10--11 target lags + hour + dayofweek + month + is\_weekend}

\medskip
\noindent
The published lag list (hours) is $24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, 168$: the same
step of day on the most recent fully observed day plus the four before it, then six daily lags
out to a week.

\medskip
\noindent
The list is instantiated per dataset by one rule: convert to steps, then shift by \textbf{whole
days} until the smallest member clears the lower bound of Eq.~\eqref{eq:window}, then drop any
member past the upper bound. Shifting by whole days preserves the structure of the list --- a
sub-daily block of five followed by daily lags out to a week --- where truncating the front
would not.

\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l c p{5.4cm} L@{}}
\toprule
 & $n$ & lag list (steps) & note \\
\midrule
\textbf{\texttt{spain}} & 11 & \texttt{24,25,26,27,28,48,72,\allowbreak 96,120,144,168}
  & \textbf{the paper's list verbatim} --- the window is exactly $[24, 168]$ \\
\textbf{\texttt{ercot}} & \textbf{10} & \texttt{48,49,50,51,52,72,96,\allowbreak 120,144,168}
  & shifted $+24$: with deadline 9, day $D{-}1$ is not fully observed, so the most recent fully observed day is $D{-}2$. \textbf{192 exceeds the upper bound 182 and is dropped} \\
\textbf{\texttt{elia}}  & \textbf{10} & \texttt{192,196,200,204,208,\allowbreak 288,384,480,576,672}
  & in steps the list is $96, 100, \ldots, 672$; its minimum 96 is below 99, so shifted one day ($+96$). \textbf{768 exceeds the upper bound 675 and is dropped} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\medskip
\noindent
So \texttt{ercot} and \texttt{elia} carry \textbf{fourteen} features, not fifteen. Both lose the
same member --- the far end of the weekly block, the least informative lag in the list --- and
both lose it to the same cause: the shift that clears the lower bound pushes the deepest lag
past the upper one. There is no shift that satisfies both ends with eleven lags, so this is
forced, not a choice.

\medskip
\noindent
This model gets \textbf{no} \texttt{known\_cols} and \textbf{no} \texttt{past\_cols} --- Li's
explicit design choice is that the base learners are generic, so that the combination step is
what is being tested. It is also why Model A is the right first build: it exercises the whole
data layer while depending on none of the column-class machinery.

\subsection{Periods and input shape}

\begin{code}
T1 = train             fit the 15 base models
T2 = first half val    report base models (the paper's Table I)
T3 = second half val   optimise the 15 weights
T4 = test              reported

X : (n_days * H, 14 or 15)      y : (n_days * H,)
\end{code}

\noindent
Li's own split is $\mathrm{T1}:\mathrm{T2}:\mathrm{T3}:\mathrm{T4} = 10{:}1{:}1{:}1$. The
configs define three periods, so T2 and T3 are the two halves of the validation days, in
order. T3 only has to pin down fifteen weights --- the paper itself uses five weeks --- so the
split is not delicate.

\subsection{Reporting space}
\label{sec:zspace}

The target is z-scored with the mean and standard deviation of the \textbf{training days}, and
Pinball, MPIW, Winkler, MAE, RMSE and MSE are reported in those units. This is what puts three
datasets of different size on one axis, and it is the scale the chapter reports in.

\medskip
\noindent
MAPE and SMAPE are the exception. They divide by the target, and the z-scored target crosses
zero, so a percentage error in z units is not a percentage error of anything; both are computed
after mapping the median and the actuals back through $(\text{mean}, \text{sd})$.

\medskip
\noindent
The same reasoning applies inside the model, to the competing MAPE-guided weighting scheme:
it is solved in physical units. The weights are unaffected by that choice --- they sum to one,
so the affine map commutes with the combination --- but the objective would otherwise be
meaningless.

\subsection{Seeds}

Three seeds by default, reported as mean and standard deviation. A seed re-seeds the
\emph{whole} pipeline: the five GPR subsamples, the five network initialisations, the five
boosters' row and column subsampling, and the QP's multi-start. Anything less would report the
spread of one run dressed up as a spread of three.

\subsection{Implementation note: the one-hot shortcut}

Both the ``best individual model'' benchmark and the four schemes' test CRPS come out of a
single $(Q, c)$ computation per period. For a one-hot weight vector $e_i$ the mean CRPS is
exactly $(Q_{ii} + c_i)/T$, so no per-model rerun is needed; for a general $w$ it is
$(w^{\top} Q w + c^{\top} w)/T$. On \texttt{elia}'s test block the $\alpha$ tensor is 129 MB,
and the naive version would build it sixteen times.

\section{Model B --- iQRNN (Zhang, Quan \& Srinivasan 2019)}

\noindent
\textbf{Built and run on all three datasets.} \texttt{src/bench/iqrnn.py} $+$
\texttt{train\_bench\_iqrnn.py}. The network, the pinball objective and the training schedule
are imported unchanged from \texttt{src/qrnn/model.py}; what is written here is the candidate
pool, which is the one part of that paper written against its own dataset. The pool figures
below are the revised ones, filtered through Eq.~\eqref{eq:window}.


\subsection{Architecture}

\begin{code}
input (103)
  |-- Linear(103 -> 500)
  |-- PReLU(500)                 per-channel learnable slope
  |-- Dropout(p)                 p = 0.5, or 0 for the -ZD variant
  |-- GaussianNoise(sd = 0.3)    training only; largest single ablation effect
  |-- Linear(500 -> 500)
  |-- PReLU(500)
  |-- Dropout(p)
  |-- GaussianNoise(sd = 0.3)
  +-- Linear(500 -> 3)           output width = the quantile set {5%, 50%, 95%}

schedule (paper Fig. 2)
    Adam, lr 1e-3, batch 168
    one `bad` counter = epochs since the best validation pinball, two rules:
        bad >= 20              stop
        every 5 bad epochs     lr *= 0.4, floored at 1e-5
    best weights restored at the end
\end{code}

\noindent
The loss is $\sum_q \mathrm{pinball}(y, \yhat_q)$. The output width \emph{is} the quantile set
and the objective \emph{is} the reported metric, so one fit produces every quantile. Two
configurations are run ($p = 0.5$ and $p = 0$), 5 seeds each, reported as mean and SD.

\subsection{Features --- candidate pool $\rightarrow$ RFE $\rightarrow$ 103}

The paper's pool is \texttt{calendar + holiday + load lags (1 d ... 5 w) + temperature
(now ... 5 w)}, 1760 candidates, cut to 100 by RFE plus 3 manual add-backs.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{None of these datasets has temperature.} Temperature is the paper's exogenous driver
known at the deadline, so it is replaced by whatever each dataset provides, under
Sec.~\ref{sec:avail}:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l l r@{}}
\toprule
 & target lags & \texttt{past\_cols} & \texttt{known\_cols} & calendar & \textbf{pool} \\
\midrule
\textbf{\texttt{elia}}  & 3{,}262 (99\ldots3360, every step) & none & $4 \times$ lag 0 & 11 & \textbf{3{,}277} \\
\textbf{\texttt{ercot}} & 803 (38\ldots840)   & 11 cols $\times$ 8 echoes $=$ 88  & none & 11 & \textbf{902} \\
\textbf{\texttt{spain}} & 817 (24\ldots840)   & 22 cols $\times$ 7 echoes $=$ 154 & $1 \times$ lag 0 & 11 & \textbf{983} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item the \textbf{target} gets a dense grid (floor $\rightarrow$ 5 weeks, every step) ---
        the paper's treatment of load;
  \item \textbf{\texttt{past\_cols}} get a daily-echo grid
        $\{\mathrm{floor}\} \cup \{\lceil \mathrm{floor}/H \rceil \cdot H + kH,\; k = 0 \ldots 6\}$.
        The paper affords a dense grid because it has one exogenous series; \texttt{spain} has
        22, and a dense grid there would be an 18{,}000-column pool;
  \item \textbf{calendar}: HoD / DoW / MoY raw codes, three sine--cosine pairs, and
        \texttt{is\_weekend}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
RFE uses \texttt{RandomForestRegressor(n\_estimators=40, criterion='absolute\_error',
max\_depth=12, max\_features=0.3, min\_samples\_leaf=20)} with \texttt{step=0.35}, on a
12{,}000-row subsample of the training rows only, cached by a signature covering the dataset,
floor, pool definition and seed.

\section{Model C --- QRA on sister forecasts: dropped}
\label{sec:qradropped}

\textbf{Not built, and not deferred.} Liu et al.'s method builds a family of sister point
forecasts and converts their DISAGREEMENT into quantiles by quantile regression. The family's
members differ only in how they treat a \textbf{temperature} recency effect, and none of these
three datasets carries a temperature column at all. Driving the family from lagged load
instead leaves all eight sisters reading one signal, so the disagreement the method exists to
convert largely disappears --- a change to the mechanism, not to the feature set. The PJM port,
still driven by real temperature, already showed the premise straining: sister dispersion of
259.6 MW against an actual error spread of 726.2 MW on \texttt{dom} (a shortfall of 2.80x, median
pairwise error correlation 0.88), and 28.9 against 199.3 on \texttt{bge} (6.89x, correlation
0.96).

\medskip
\noindent
Each dataset then breaks it a second time, differently. The estimation window must be full
before the first forecast origin, and Liu's family is two windows (2 y and 3 y) crossed with
four selection schemes:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l r c c l@{}}
\toprule
 & history before validation & 2 y & 3 y & consequence \\
\midrule
\texttt{spain} & 2.38 y & yes & no  & 8 sisters collapse to 4 \\
\texttt{ercot} & 1.14 y & no  & no  & no feasible window at all \\
\texttt{elia}  & 6.13 y & yes & yes & family intact, but see below \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
\texttt{elia}'s 15-minute granularity puts the stage-1 design at 2953 columns; one
\texttt{lstsq} was measured at 26.2 s on this node, so daily re-estimation over 1494 forecast
days is 11{,}952 fits and 87 h. Two concessions (hour-of-day rather than step-of-day
interactions, weekly rather than daily re-estimation) bring it to 2.8 h.

\medskip
\noindent
So the cheapest honest version would be a relabelled method (``QRA applied to an
autoregressive sister family'') with a different deviation on every dataset and an empty cell
for \texttt{ercot}. Models A and B each survive the missing temperature intact --- Li's
combination step and Zhang's training machinery depend on no particular exogenous variable ---
so the baseline set is those two. The PJM implementation
(\texttt{src/quantile/\{qra,sisters,design,qra\_plots\}.py}, \texttt{train\_qra.py}) has been
deleted from the repository; \texttt{src/quantile/\_\_init\_\_.py} records why.

\section{Metrics}

\noindent
\textbf{Built} --- \texttt{src/bench/metrics.py}.
Quantile set $\{5\%, 50\%, 95\%\}$, interval $[\yhat_{0.05}, \yhat_{0.95}]$, so
$\alpha = 0.10$. Write $\ell$ and $u$ for the interval bounds and index the test block by day
$d$ and step $h$, over $N$ days $\times$ $H$ steps:

\begin{align}
  \mathrm{Pinball} &= \frac{1}{|Q|\,N H} \sum_{q \in Q} \sum_{d,h}
      \max\!\Big\{ (q-1)\big(y_{d,h} - \yhat_{d,h,q}\big),\;
                    q\big(y_{d,h} - \yhat_{d,h,q}\big) \Big\} \\[2pt]
  \mathrm{PICP} &= \frac{1}{N H} \sum_{d,h} \ind\{\ell_{d,h} \leq y_{d,h} \leq u_{d,h}\} \\[2pt]
  \mathrm{MPIW} &= \frac{1}{N H} \sum_{d,h} \big(u_{d,h} - \ell_{d,h}\big) \\[2pt]
  \mathrm{Winkler} &= \frac{1}{N H} \sum_{d,h} \Big[
      \big(u_{d,h} - \ell_{d,h}\big)
      + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}\big(\ell_{d,h} - y_{d,h}\big)\ind\{y_{d,h} < \ell_{d,h}\} \nonumber \\
      &\hspace{6.2em}
      + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}\big(y_{d,h} - u_{d,h}\big)\ind\{y_{d,h} > u_{d,h}\} \Big]
\end{align}

\noindent
Point metrics use the median $\yhat_{0.50}$:
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{MAE}  &= \frac{1}{N H} \sum_{d,h} \big| \yhat_{d,h,0.5} - y_{d,h} \big| \\
  \mathrm{RMSE} &= \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N H} \sum_{d,h} \big( \yhat_{d,h,0.5} - y_{d,h} \big)^2 } \\
  \mathrm{MSE}  &= \frac{1}{N H} \sum_{d,h} \big( \yhat_{d,h,0.5} - y_{d,h} \big)^2 \\
  \mathrm{MAPE} &= \frac{100}{N H} \sum_{d,h}
                   \frac{\big| \yhat_{d,h,0.5} - y_{d,h} \big|}{\big| y_{d,h} \big|} \\
  \mathrm{SMAPE} &= \frac{100}{N H} \sum_{d,h}
                   \frac{2\,\big| \yhat_{d,h,0.5} - y_{d,h} \big|}
                        {\big| \yhat_{d,h,0.5} \big| + \big| y_{d,h} \big|}
\end{align}

\noindent
\textbf{Not reused from \texttt{qeval}.} Its \texttt{pinball}, \texttt{winkler} and
\texttt{coverage\_report} all index into a module-level \texttt{QUANTILES = np.arange(1, 100)},
so they can score a 99-level grid and nothing else --- including, in \texttt{winkler}, looking
up the band by integer percentile. Every function here takes the levels as an argument instead,
which is also what lets the same code score a dense grid when a reference CRPS is wanted.

\medskip
\noindent
Units follow Sec.~\ref{sec:zspace}: everything above is on the z-scored target except MAPE and
SMAPE, which are computed in physical units. A crossing-rate diagnostic is reported alongside;
every model here sorts its output, so a non-zero value means a bug upstream rather than a
property of the forecast.

\medskip
\noindent
The test block reshapes to $(N, H)$ exactly --- no dataset has DST or missing steps, and the
day-set rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:dayset} admits only complete days --- so per-step and per-day
breakdowns fall out of the same array with no partial day to drop.

\section{Known problems}

\subsection{The forecast columns are shifted relative to their timestamps}
\label{sec:align}

\begin{codetiny}
spain  total load forecast     lag 0   8.76%  ->  +24 steps    1.11%
ercot  ERC_Load                lag 0   4.45%  ->  +24 steps    2.46%
ercot  ERC_Wind                lag 0  70.32%  ->  +24 steps   17.25%
ercot  ERC_Solar               lag 0  49.07%  ->  +24 steps   29.38%
elia   Day-ahead 6PM forecast  lag 0  13.04%  ->  +24 steps (= 6 h)    2.63%
elia   Week-ahead forecast     lag 0  15.24%  -> +168 steps (= 42 h)   2.94%
elia   Most recent forecast    lag 0   1.92%  ->   +0 steps  (not shifted)
\end{codetiny}

\noindent
It is the forecast columns and not the actuals that moved: \texttt{spain}'s summed generation
correlates best with \texttt{total load actual} at lag 0 (0.8972, against 0.6335 at lag 24).
On \texttt{elia} the shifts are 24 and 168 --- the \emph{hourly} lag constants applied to a
15-minute series, giving 6 h and 42 h instead of 24 h and 168 h --- and \texttt{Most recent
forecast} was not shifted at all, which an intentional scheme would not do.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Resolved as: leave it.} Per your instruction the columns are used at lag 0, unshifted.
Running as configured is not a leak, only a degradation, and the cost lands hardest on
\texttt{ercot}, whose target is \textbf{net load} and whose wind and solar day-ahead forecasts
are its main exogenous signal. This affects Models B and C only --- Model A uses no exogenous
column at all, so it is unaffected either way. The probe and the per-column shift map are
therefore deferred with the rest of the column-class machinery.

\subsection{Model A's combination may not beat its best member}
\label{sec:combloss}

Open, and the first thing to look at once Model A runs. On the PJM port of this same paper the
CRPS-guided combination \emph{lost} to the best individual base model, with all fifteen weights
non-zero, none above 0.20, and a result within 0.2\% of the simple average --- the signature of
a QP sitting on a flat or local solution rather than of a real finding, since the paper's whole
claim is that $w_i = 0$ drops models.

\medskip
\noindent
That was never diagnosed. It matters here because the convexity certificate
described above is computed on every run: if it comes back positive, the solution
is global and a flat weight vector is genuinely what the data says; if it comes back negative,
the multi-start is the only defence and the result should not be trusted without more starts.

\medskip
\noindent
\texttt{spain} is the clean test, because its lag list is the paper's verbatim --- if the
combination underperforms \emph{there}, the cause is the implementation, not the adaptation.

\subsection{iQRNN stops after three to five epochs on \texttt{spain} and \texttt{ercot}}
\label{sec:earlystop}

Open. With \texttt{stop\_patience} at 20, runs that end at epoch 23--25 had their best
validation score around epoch 3--5. On \texttt{spain} an epoch is 124 batches, so the network
saw roughly 600 updates. \texttt{elia} trains for 58--61 epochs and scores far better relative
to Model A (pinball 0.0433 against 0.0573) where \texttt{spain} scores worse (0.1008 against
0.0867), which is consistent with the two small datasets stopping before they have converged
rather than after.

\subsection{\texttt{ercot} has 9{,}984 training samples against the paper's 42{,}984}
\label{sec:ercotsize}

The $103 \rightarrow 500 \rightarrow 500$ network carries ${\sim}350$k parameters. The paper's
own size sweep found 500 sufficient at 43k samples; 200--300 is probably right here. A short
width sweep on the validation block before fixing the number is cheap (\texttt{ercot} trains in
${\sim}25$ s per run).

\subsection{The three datasets do not pose the same task}

\texttt{elia}'s \texttt{known\_cols} contains the official day-ahead load forecast;
\texttt{spain}'s and \texttt{ercot}'s do not (\texttt{spain}'s \texttt{total load forecast} is
excluded from both lists, which looks deliberate --- including it would reduce the task to
copying the TSO). \texttt{elia}'s scores will look better and part of that is the TSO's, not
the model's. Printed in each dataset's output header.

\subsection{Feature sets are not symmetric across models}

Following each paper, density gets no exogenous variables at all (Li's explicit design
choice), while iQRNN's pool does --- so on \texttt{elia} iQRNN sees the official forecast and
density does not. Faithful, but it needs saying in the results. The alternative, giving all
three the same feature set, would take density away from its published specification.

\section{Contract tests}
\label{sec:tests}

\texttt{test/test\_bench\_density.py}, in the style of
\texttt{test/test\_qrnn\_features.py} --- five things that would produce a plausible-looking but
wrong table rather than raise. \textbf{All five pass.}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{day set} --- \texttt{(train, val, test)} day counts equal the chapter's
        $869/292/291$, $416/142/140$, $2239/748/746$ for the three datasets;
  \item \textbf{lag window} --- every lag lies inside Eq.~\eqref{eq:window} on all three
        datasets, and at least ten survive;
  \item \textbf{lag alignment} --- every \texttt{lag\{k\}} column equals the target series at
        exactly $k$ steps earlier, and no lag taken from the target day's first step reaches
        past the forecast deadline;
  \item \textbf{pinball} --- equals
        $\max\{\tau(y - \yhat),\,(\tau-1)(y - \yhat)\}$ elementwise, to machine precision;
  \item \textbf{no crossing} --- the mixture quantiles at $\{0.05, 0.50, 0.95\}$ are strictly
        increasing, so the grid inversion is fine at three levels as it was at ninety-nine.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent
Two tests from the draft are gone with the code they covered: the lazy-matrix equivalence test
(no lazy matrix) and the \texttt{qeval} agreement test (\texttt{qeval} is replaced, not reused,
so agreeing with it is not the property wanted --- agreeing with the \emph{definition} is).
